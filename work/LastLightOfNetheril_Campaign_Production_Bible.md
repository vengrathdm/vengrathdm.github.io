# The Last Light of Netheril
## Campaign Production Bible — Purpose, Requirements, and Editorial Standards

**Document purpose:** Establish the creative, structural, mechanical, visual, and production requirements for a 256-page Dungeons & Dragons campaign, without deciding the campaign's plot, villain, mystery solution, sequence of revelations, or ending.

This document is the governing specification for later writing. Its purpose is to ensure that chapters belong to the same campaign, support the same play experience, use consistent rules and terminology, and achieve page density comparable to a professionally published WotC adventure.

The production target is **close to official D&D adventure-book density and usability**, not a reproduction of WotC's proprietary prose. We will use original writing, familiar tabletop-adventure conventions, and consistent Homebrewery-compatible Markdown.

---

# 1. Campaign Identity

**Title:** *The Last Light of Netheril*  
**Format:** Full-length, hardcover-scale fantasy campaign manuscript.  
**Target length:** 256 designed pages.  
**Ruleset:** Revised D&D 5e rules published in 2024.  
**Primary format:** One structured Markdown manuscript, suitable for later Homebrewery rendering and conversion into a polished campaign book.

**Core campaign architecture:** A dual-timeline campaign in which each player creates and plays two distinct characters: one in ancient Netheril and one in present-day Faerûn. The timelines are active campaigns, not flashbacks, historical interludes, or background lore. The two storylines eventually intersect in a shared final chapter.

**Audience:** A Dungeon Master who wants to run a substantial, high-stakes campaign with clear instructions, useful reference material, meaningful character decisions, and enough encounter detail to run sessions without inventing essential connective material.

**Design ambition:** The campaign should feel like a complete published adventure, not a collection of ideas, lore entries, encounter prompts, or disconnected locations.

## 1.1 Creative Promise

The campaign should deliver four experiences:

1. **Ancient grandeur:** Players experience Netheril as a living, powerful, sophisticated civilization—not merely a ruin whose significance is explained by modern characters.
2. **Present-day discovery:** Players investigate the surviving consequences of Netheril and form interpretations that can change how they understand its history.
3. **Cross-timeline consequence:** Actions in the ancient timeline have specific, intelligible effects in the present. Discoveries in the present can change how players understand decisions made in the past.
4. **Shared culmination:** Both generations of characters matter in the finale. The conclusion depends on what each group can uniquely contribute.

These are requirements for the eventual plot, not decisions about what that plot will be.

## 1.2 What This Campaign Is Not

The manuscript must not become:

- A lore encyclopedia that delays playable content.
- A linear novel with encounters inserted between chapters.
- A historical reconstruction in which ancient characters have no meaningful agency because the past is already known.
- A mystery that depends on a single missed clue or required NPC.
- A campaign in which present-day characters exist only to explain the ancient characters' accomplishments.
- A campaign in which ancient characters exist only to provide spectacle for the present-day characters.
- A sequence of combats whose outcomes have no effect on the world, relationships, available resources, or later choices.
- A sourcebook whose setting information is disconnected from the adventures that use it.

Setting material, narrative, mechanics, and player decisions must reinforce one another.

# 2. Player and Campaign Structure

## 2.1 Two Characters per Player

Every player creates two player characters (PCs):

- **Netherese PC:** A character participating in ancient Netheril, beginning at 11th level.
- **Faerûnian PC:** A character operating in the present day, beginning at 5th level.

The two characters are separate people with separate character sheets, capabilities, relationships, possessions, and immediate goals. They must not be treated as two versions of one character unless a future plot decision explicitly establishes such a connection.

| Design dimension | Ancient Netheril | Present-day Faerûn |
|---|---|---|
| Starting level | 11 | 5 |
| Campaign role | Direct participation in a powerful ancient civilization | Investigation, exploration, interpretation, and response |
| Power fantasy | High-level capability, magical infrastructure, political and arcane stakes | Growing competence, dangerous discoveries, tactical ingenuity, and consequences |
| Main dramatic tension | Power, ambition, obligation, risk, and the cost of decisions | Uncertainty, incomplete evidence, competing interpretations, and the implications of recovery |
| Required agency | Decisions change the ancient timeline and what survives | Decisions change what is understood, recovered, preserved, or done |
| Narrative value | Firsthand experience of events later generations may misunderstand | Access to evidence and context unavailable to the ancient characters |

These are design tendencies, not restrictions on character class, personality, alignment, or approach.

## 2.2 Level Progression

The following chapter-level schedule is fixed for production planning.

| Chapter | Timeline | Starting level | Level after chapter |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| I | Present-day Faerûn | 5 | 6 |
| II | Ancient Netheril | 11 | 12 |
| III | Present-day Faerûn | 6 | 7 |
| IV | Ancient Netheril | 12 | 13 |
| V | Present-day Faerûn | 7 | 8 |
| VI | Ancient Netheril | 13 | 14 |
| VII | Place Between Times | 8 and 14 | Campaign finale |

The Faerûnian characters gain a level after each of their first three chapters. The Netherese characters gain a level after each of their first three chapters. The finale begins with both sets of characters at their respective levels.

The manuscript must state clearly when level advancement occurs and how it is awarded. Chapter transitions must not create ambiguity about whether a character has advanced before the next encounter.

## 2.3 Dual-Timeline Continuity

The two timelines must remain coherent at three levels:

- **Historical continuity:** The ancient timeline follows an internally consistent sequence of events, regardless of what later generations believe happened.
- **Causal continuity:** Ancient decisions create defined consequences in the present. These may be direct, delayed, altered by intervening history, or misunderstood, but their causal relationship must be traceable by the author and DM.
- **Interpretive continuity:** Evidence in the present may revise the meaning of an ancient event without arbitrarily changing the event itself. A later revelation should clarify, complicate, or recontextualize established information rather than retroactively invalidate it without preparation.

Every cross-timeline dependency must be recorded in an internal continuity ledger. The ledger is a production requirement; it need not appear in full in the published book.

## 2.4 Player Knowledge and Character Knowledge

The manuscript must distinguish what the players know from what each group of characters knows. It should not assume that a player who participated in an ancient chapter will automatically make the correct inference in a present-day chapter.

Where player knowledge could bypass a meaningful discovery, preserve the value of character actions without arbitrarily forbidding players from using what they know. A player may recognize a pattern before their character has the necessary evidence; the game should still provide meaningful ways for the character to test, substantiate, or act on that insight.

# 3. Final Chapter and Shared Play Requirements

Chapter VII takes place in the **Place Between Times**, where the two eras intersect. It is a shared chapter, not a separate epilogue for either timeline.

Each player controls both of their characters during this chapter. This defining campaign feature must be supported by encounter design rather than left to the DM to improvise.

The finale must meet these requirements:

- Both character groups have necessary capabilities or knowledge.
- Each group can affect objectives that the other group cannot resolve in the same way.
- The ancient characters' higher level does not make the present-day characters redundant.
- The present-day characters' interpretive knowledge does not reduce the ancient characters to muscle or spectacle.
- The encounter structure avoids simply doubling each player's turns without accounting for the additional cognitive load.
- The DM can track both parties' actions, resources, positioning, and consequences without excessive bookkeeping.
- The finale supports meaningful decisions under pressure, not merely a sequence of required skill checks.
- Outcomes reflect characters' actions and prior campaign choices where those choices have established consequences.

The exact final threat, setting details, objectives, and resolution are intentionally undecided in this document.

# 4. Rules and Mechanical Standards

The manuscript is written for the **2024 revised fifth-edition rules**. The 2014 rules must not be the default reference when a rule has been revised.

## 4.1 Rules Authority

Use the 2024 Player's Handbook, Dungeon Master's Guide, and Monster Manual as the primary rules references for their respective subjects. Where the campaign introduces original mechanics, identify them as campaign-specific rules and provide complete instructions.

Every mechanical element must be one of the following:

1. A rule used as written from the 2024 ruleset.
2. A clearly identified adaptation of an existing rule.
3. An original campaign mechanic with its own complete text.

Do not silently blend different rules editions. If a legacy monster, magic item, or spell is used, verify that its wording and interaction with the 2024 rules are appropriate, and explain any necessary changes.

## 4.2 Rules-Writing Requirements

Every new mechanic must answer the questions relevant to its use:

- What triggers it?
- Who or what is affected?
- What action, bonus action, reaction, or other game procedure is required?
- What range, area, duration, or target restriction applies?
- What saving throw, attack roll, ability check, or other resolution method is used?
- What happens on success and failure?
- Can the effect be repeated, ended, resisted, dispelled, or interrupted?
- Does it require concentration, a resource, a rest, or a limited use?
- How does it interact with conditions and other common rules?
- Is its intended use clear to the DM during play?

Include the details necessary for the mechanic to function, but do not omit details that change its adjudication.

## 4.3 Encounter Design

Encounters must be designed for the actual character levels listed in the chapter, not for a generic party of adventurers.

Each encounter should account for intended party size where relevant, enemy capabilities, environmental conditions, available resources, likely player approaches, and consequences of success or failure. A combat encounter is not complete merely because creatures have been selected.

For major encounters, the DM-facing text should make clear:

- Why the encounter occurs.
- What the opposition wants and how it behaves.
- What the environment contributes.
- What changes during the encounter, if anything.
- What happens if the characters win, lose, withdraw, negotiate, or bypass it.
- Which elements are essential to the adventure and which are optional.

Avoid assuming that combat is always the intended or best resolution. Where the fiction supports negotiation, stealth, retreat, investigation, or environmental problem-solving, provide enough information to make those approaches viable.

## 4.4 Original Content

Original monsters, NPC stat blocks, spells, magic items, hazards, and other mechanics must use consistent terminology and formatting. They should have a clear purpose in the adventure and be checked for interactions with the 2024 rules.

Do not create a new rule merely to express something that an existing rule already handles adequately. Conversely, do not use vague narrative language where a precise mechanical rule is required.

# 5. Narrative and Adventure-Writing Standards

The campaign must read like a professionally edited tabletop adventure. The writing should be evocative, efficient, concrete, and operational for the DM.

## 5.1 Separate DM Information from Player-Facing Text

The manuscript must distinguish:

- **DM-facing instructions:** what the DM needs to understand, prepare, decide, or track.
- **Player-facing read-aloud text:** what can be read or paraphrased to players when a particular situation occurs.
- **Reference information:** rules, statistics, lists, clues, tables, maps, and other material consulted during play.

Read-aloud passages should convey what characters can immediately perceive. They should not disclose hidden information, dictate a character's emotional reaction, assume a particular action, or explain a mystery that characters have not yet discovered.

DM-facing prose should explain how to run a situation rather than simply restate what the players can see.

## 5.2 Operational Clarity

The DM should be able to answer the following questions without reconstructing the author's intent:

- Where are the characters?
- What can they perceive?
- What is happening now?
- What can they interact with?
- What information is hidden, and how can it be discovered?
- Which creatures or NPCs are present, and what do they want?
- What happens if the characters do something unexpected?
- What information or consequence should be carried into a later chapter?

When the answer depends on a judgment call, explain the principle behind that judgment. Do not script every possible player action, but provide enough structure for the DM to respond consistently.

## 5.3 Branching and Consequences

Player choices should change something meaningful. A choice may affect relationships, resources, access, risk, information, timing, the state of a location, or the consequences inherited by the other timeline.

The manuscript must not create false choices whose outcomes are identical unless the point is explicitly to demonstrate a constraint or trade-off. Nor should every choice create a large branching plot that doubles the required writing. Use manageable consequences that can be tracked and incorporated into later chapters.

A campaign-level decision should be recorded with:

- The triggering choice.
- The possible outcomes.
- The immediate effect.
- The later effect, if any.
- The information the DM needs to track.
- How the outcome is communicated to the players.

## 5.4 Clues and Discoveries

Mystery-related content must be robust to missed checks, ignored rooms, defeated NPCs, and unexpected routes through the adventure.

Essential progress should not depend on one isolated clue. If a conclusion is necessary to continue, plan multiple ways to obtain or corroborate the relevant information. Different paths may cost different resources or reveal different context, but a failed roll should not accidentally destroy the campaign's only route forward.

Checks should determine meaningful uncertainty, the cost of obtaining information, the speed of discovery, or the quality of an outcome—not arbitrarily decide whether the adventure can continue.

## 5.5 Tone

The tone is mythic, ancient, mysterious, tragic, and awe-inspiring. It should communicate the scale and magical sophistication of Netheril while keeping the experience grounded in the characters' immediate choices and circumstances.

Avoid treating Netheril as a simple contrast between a glorious past and an inferior present. Both eras should have beauty, danger, internal contradictions, competent people, and genuine stakes.

Use concrete details and selective evocative language. Do not rely on a continuous stream of grandiose adjectives to make every room, object, or NPC feel significant. Scale and wonder are more effective when contrasted with ordinary human concerns and specific consequences.

# 6. Production Density and Page-Budget Requirements

This section governs the manuscript's physical density. Its purpose is to avoid producing a 256-page outline that looks complete by page count but contains far less usable content than a published campaign.

## 6.1 Reference Density

The current reference figures supplied for the project are:

| Metric | Reference value |
|---|---:|
| Average words per line | 10.45 |
| Lines per full-text column | 51.2 |
| Words per full-text column | 535.04 |
| Total words across 221 text pages | 149,818 |
| Average words per text page | 677.91 |
| Full-text-column equivalents per text page | 1.267 |
| Full-text columns possible per printed page | 0–2 |

The calculations are:

- 10.45 words/line × 51.2 lines/column = **535.04 words/column**.
- 149,818 words ÷ 221 text pages ≈ **677.91 words/text page**.
- 677.91 words/page ÷ 535.04 words/column ≈ **1.267 full-text-column equivalents per text page**.

These values are the project's current working benchmark. The exact reference book, edition, and page-counting method still need to be documented before the figures can be attributed conclusively to a particular WotC publication.

**The key production rule:** do not equate a page with a fixed number of words. A full-page illustration may contain no prose; a two-column page may contain substantial text; and a mixed page may contain prose, tables, callouts, stat blocks, or a map.

## 6.2 Column-Equivalent Budgeting

For planning, use a **full-text-column equivalent (FTCE)** as an approximate measure of prose capacity.

- One full column = approximately 535 words.
- Two full columns = approximately 1,070 words.
- One half-column = approximately 268 words.
- A page with no text = zero prose-column equivalents.

This is a planning unit, not a claim that every layout will hold the exact same number of words. Headings, tables, stat blocks, and callout boxes change the layout, and a designed page may not use its available space efficiently.

For each planned page, estimate the amount of space occupied by full-text-column equivalents. A page may be classified as an illustration page, a map page, a mixed-content page, a full-text page, or another useful layout type. Track the category rather than forcing every page into a prose quota.

## 6.3 Theoretical Maximum Versus Realistic Book

At two full-text columns per page, 256 pages would allow:

256 × 2 × 535.04 = **273,940.48 words**.

That is a theoretical full-text ceiling under the current assumptions. It is **not** the target word count for the campaign. A real adventure book must reserve space for maps, illustrations, chapter openers, stat blocks, tables, and other visual or reference material.

The manuscript's actual word target should be calculated after the page budget is divided into layout categories. The target must reflect the intended visual design, not merely the maximum amount of text that could fit.

## 6.4 Density Targets for Later Drafting

When drafting begins, each chapter must have two targets:

1. **Designed-page target:** how many pages the chapter is expected to occupy after layout.
2. **Content-volume target:** how much usable text and reference content is required, measured in approximate column equivalents and word counts.

These targets should be reviewed together. A chapter that reaches its word target but requires twice the planned page count is not complete from a production standpoint. Similarly, a chapter that reaches its page count through large headings and whitespace but lacks enough encounter guidance is not complete.

The manuscript should not be padded to reach a target. Every section should provide information that helps the DM run the adventure, helps players make decisions, supports the setting, or enables the campaign's mechanics.

## 6.5 Page-Level Content Classification

| Page type | Primary purpose | Density expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Chapter opener | Establish chapter identity, mood, and essential framing | Low to moderate |
| Adventure prose | Explain events, locations, situations, and choices | Moderate to high |
| Read-aloud and descriptive page | Deliver player-facing description with supporting DM information | Variable |
| Encounter page | Make a scene or encounter runnable | Moderate to high |
| Stat-block page | Present mechanical information for rapid reference | Variable; do not use prose targets |
| Table/reference page | Provide reusable information in a compact form | Variable |
| Map/illustration page | Communicate spatial, narrative, or visual information | Low prose density may be appropriate |
| Appendix page | Supply rules, NPCs, items, summaries, and other lookup material | Moderate to high |
| Mixed page | Combine two or more of the above functions | Variable |

The final design can combine categories. These labels support planning and auditing; they do not impose a rigid layout on the designer.

## 6.6 Word-Count Accounting

The production process must distinguish at least three counts:

- **Raw manuscript words:** all words in the source, including headings, tables, stat blocks, callouts, and notes.
- **Adventure-content words:** usable text intended for the published book, excluding internal production comments and discarded drafts.
- **Designed-page words:** words that will actually appear in the laid-out book, measured after repeated elements and production notes have been removed.

For density comparisons, state whether the count includes table contents, stat blocks, captions, headers, footers, and page numbers. Use the same counting convention for the reference sample and the new manuscript.

Do not use an automated word count without checking whether the tool counts Markdown syntax, repeated headings, or internal comments as published text.

# 7. The 256-Page Book Architecture

The overall page allocation is a fixed planning constraint. It does not prescribe the plot or require each chapter to use the same internal structure.

| Section | Planned pages |
|---|---:|
| Front matter and campaign guide | 24 |
| Chapter I — Faerûn, levels 5–6 | 24 |
| Chapter II — Netheril, levels 11–12 | 28 |
| Chapter III — Faerûn, levels 6–7 | 24 |
| Chapter IV — Netheril, levels 12–13 | 28 |
| Chapter V — Faerûn, levels 7–8 | 24 |
| Chapter VI — Netheril, levels 13–14 | 32 |
| Chapter VII — Place Between Times | 36 |
| Appendices | 36 |
| **Total** | **256** |

These are design allocations, not guaranteed final pagination. If the eventual layout requires changes, record the reason and identify what content is being moved, reduced, or expanded.

## 7.1 Front Matter and Campaign Guide

The front matter must give the DM enough information to understand and prepare the campaign before running the first session. It should include the campaign's premise, two-character structure, level progression, timeline conventions, major campaign procedures, tone and safety considerations where appropriate, and guidance for managing the two sets of characters.

It should not disclose every mystery or dictate a single correct interpretation of the story. The DM needs to know how the campaign operates, which facts must remain consistent, and how to track cross-timeline consequences.

## 7.2 Chapters I–VI

Each chapter must function as a playable unit while contributing to the wider campaign. Chapter length is determined by the content needed to make that unit complete, not by a requirement to fill every page with prose.

A chapter should have a clear beginning, a playable sequence of situations, meaningful decisions, a defined set of possible outcomes, and a usable transition to the next stage of the campaign. The exact events, locations, antagonists, and revelations are intentionally left open.

## 7.3 Chapter VII

The final chapter has the largest individual chapter allocation because it must accommodate the shared setting, both character groups, their combined mechanics, and the campaign's resolution. It must be designed to support simultaneous two-character play, with explicit guidance for encounter pacing, objective tracking, and spotlight balance.

The page allocation does not imply that the finale must be a single long combat. Its structure will be decided during plot development.

## 7.4 Appendices

The appendices should reduce the need for the DM to reconstruct information from earlier chapters. They may contain original creatures, NPC statistics, magic items, hazards, rules modules, maps, handouts, campaign trackers, or reference tables where the completed adventure requires them.

Only include material that supports play or improves usability. Appendices should not become a dumping ground for material that should have been explained in the relevant chapter.

# 8. Markdown and Homebrewery Source Conventions

The manuscript must remain readable as plain Markdown while preserving the formatting conventions used by the Homebrewery renderer. Markdown syntax is the source language; the rendered book is the presentation layer.

## 8.1 Required Structural Syntax

Use the following hierarchy consistently:

```markdown
# Chapter title

## Major section

### Subsection

#### Specific location, encounter, or reference topic
```

Use `#` for chapter-level titles, `##` for major chapter sections, `###` for subsections, and `####` for specific topics beneath a subsection. Do not skip heading levels simply to obtain a preferred font size.

Tables should use standard Markdown syntax. Use tables when they make information easier to scan, compare, or reference during play. Do not put long narrative passages into tables merely to save vertical space.

## 8.2 Homebrewery Blocks

Use the existing project conventions for callouts and descriptive passages.

**Note block:**

```markdown
{{note
**Running This Section.** Explain the practical guidance the DM needs
before running the scene, encounter, or subsystem.
}}
```

**Descriptive block:**

```markdown
{{descriptive
The characters see, hear, smell, or otherwise perceive the environment.
Keep this passage suitable for reading aloud or paraphrasing at the table.
}}
```

The syntax must be consistent across the book. Blocks should contain content that fits their purpose. A `note` should not be a generic container for every type of information, and a `descriptive` block should not conceal rules the DM needs to adjudicate.

These examples establish formatting, not final campaign prose.

## 8.3 Content Conventions

Use bold text for important terms, names, mechanical labels, and short scan points. Use italics for emphasis, titles, and terminology where it improves readability. Use tables for concise comparisons and repeated structured data. Use lists for genuinely enumerated procedures, options, and checklists.

Do not use formatting merely to make every paragraph visually distinct. Excessive bolding, decorative headings, and unnecessary callouts reduce the contrast between ordinary text and information that genuinely needs attention.

## 8.4 Read-Aloud Convention

Player-facing text must be visually distinct from DM-facing instructions. The manuscript should make it immediately apparent when the DM can read a passage aloud and when the text instead contains secrets, procedures, tactics, or interpretation.

If a descriptive passage contains details that are not immediately perceivable, separate those details into DM-facing text rather than mixing them into the read-aloud.

## 8.5 Tables and Mechanical References

Tables must have consistent column labels, terminology, capitalization, and units. Each table must make sense without requiring the reader to infer what a column means from an earlier page.

For tables likely to be consulted repeatedly during play, keep entries concise and put the most useful information first. Long explanations belong in prose with a clear cross-reference.

## 8.6 Source Integrity

Do not insert layout-dependent page references into the manuscript before pagination is stable. During drafting, use stable chapter and section identifiers or explicit editorial placeholders. Replace them with verified page references only after the final layout has been generated.

Do not use a page number guessed from the 256-page budget as if it were a confirmed reference.

# 9. Editorial Voice and Presentation

The desired result is a professional, DM-oriented adventure with the clarity, navigability, and practical utility expected from a published D&D campaign.

This is a functional and editorial target, not an instruction to reproduce WotC's distinctive proprietary wording. All prose, encounters, NPCs, mechanics, descriptions, and campaign material must be original.

## 9.1 Prose Principles

The writing should be:

- **Direct:** tell the DM what is true, what happens, and what they should do.
- **Specific:** prefer concrete details over vague atmosphere.
- **Efficient:** avoid repeating information unless repetition improves usability.
- **Evocative:** use selected sensory details and strong imagery to establish tone.
- **Actionable:** distinguish what the DM must prepare, what can happen, and what changes in response to the players.
- **Consistent:** use the same terms for the same people, objects, rules, and procedures throughout the book.

Avoid both extremes: bare notes that force the DM to write the adventure themselves, and elaborate prose that hides the information required to run it.

## 9.2 Instructions to the DM

Instructions should identify the relevant action and its context. A useful instruction explains when a creature acts, what it wants, what triggers a change in behavior, and what happens if the characters interfere. It should not merely state that the scene is tense or that the creature is dangerous.

Where an instruction is conditional, state the condition. Where a rule is optional, label it as optional. Where the DM must make a judgment, explain what the judgment should preserve or accomplish.

## 9.3 Terminology and Copy-Editing

Use consistent capitalization for proper nouns, named abilities, chapter titles, conditions, and other rules terms. Follow the 2024 rules terminology. Maintain a glossary of campaign-specific names and terms to prevent spelling variants and inconsistent capitalization.

The final copy-edit must check grammar, punctuation, duplicated words, broken Markdown, malformed Homebrewery blocks, incomplete tables, stale cross-references, and terminology drift.

# 10. Visual Identity and Art Direction

The campaign's visual language is **mythic Art Deco, minimalist cosmic fantasy, and monumental geometric illustration**.

The book should feel ancient, regal, mysterious, tragic, and awe-inspiring. The visual design should communicate the scale and magical sophistication of Netheril without making every page visually crowded or excessively ornate.

## 10.1 Core Palette

| Color | Hex | Primary role |
|---|---|---|
| Midnight Obsidian | `#10152F` | Dark backgrounds, voids, silhouettes |
| Netheril Blue | `#1555A0` | Arcane structures and magical forms |
| Last Light Cream | `#F9DFA0` | Light, highlights, important text accents |
| Solar Amber | `#FF8A24` | Magical illumination and focal accents |
| Fading Ember | `#F05B28` | Danger, heat, destruction, urgency |
| Astral Violet | `#493478` | Cosmic depth and unusual magic |

These colors are the core palette, not a requirement that every illustration use all six. Contrast, legibility, and the purpose of the image take precedence.

## 10.2 Shape Language

Prefer bold silhouettes, crisp edges, simplified architecture, strong geometry, clean negative space, and clear focal points. Floating cities, celestial discs, orbital rings, falling spires, monumental profiles, and descending beams are useful visual motifs when they fit the content.

Avoid defaulting to photorealism, painterly gradients, highly intricate architecture, dense ornamental detail, or noisy textures that obscure the composition. Detail should serve recognition, mood, or meaning.

## 10.3 Layout Principles

Each page should have a clear visual hierarchy. Headings, callouts, tables, maps, illustrations, and body text should work together rather than compete for attention.

The design should balance dense reference pages with pages that need space for maps, illustrations, or dramatic presentation. White space is a legitimate design tool, but it must not be used to disguise a lack of substantive content.

## 10.4 Typography

The working type direction is:

- **Cinzel** for major titles and chapter headings.
- **Source Sans 3** for body text and practical reference material.
- **Cinzel Decorative** for limited ceremonial or emblematic uses.
- **Cormorant Garamond** as an alternative for selected literary or atmospheric applications, if needed.

Typography must remain readable at printed size. Decorative faces should not be used for dense mechanical information or long paragraphs.

## 10.5 Art as Part of the Page Budget

Illustrations and maps must be accounted for during production planning. They are not additions that can be inserted without affecting pagination.

Every planned image should have a defined purpose: establish a location, communicate spatial information, depict a creature or NPC, clarify a magical phenomenon, create a chapter transition, or support the book's visual identity. Repeated decorative images should not displace essential adventure content.

# 11. Campaign Continuity and Internal Reference Systems

The campaign requires a stronger continuity process than a conventional single-timeline adventure because decisions and discoveries may carry consequences across chapters and eras.

These records should be maintained during writing, even if they are never included in the final book.

## 11.1 Canon Ledger

Maintain one authoritative entry for every important fact established by the manuscript.

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Identifier | Stable, unique reference |
| Fact | What is true in the campaign |
| Era | Ancient Netheril, present-day Faerûn, or both |
| First appearance | Chapter and section |
| Known by | Which characters or groups know it |
| Evidence | What supports the fact in the fiction |
| Dependencies | Other facts that rely on it |
| Status | Established, provisional, or unresolved |

A provisional idea must not silently become established canon. If a later draft changes an established fact, the change must be deliberate and all dependent material must be reviewed.

## 11.2 Cross-Timeline Consequence Ledger

Every significant ancient action with a later consequence must have a corresponding record.

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Ancient action | What the Netherese characters did or chose |
| Immediate result | What changes in the ancient timeline |
| Historical persistence | What survives, decays, changes, or is forgotten |
| Present-day manifestation | How the result affects the modern timeline |
| Discoverability | How present-day characters can learn about it |
| Player agency | What the modern characters can do about it |
| Tracking requirement | What the DM must record during play |

Not every ancient action needs to change the future. The purpose is to make consequential actions traceable, not to force every scene into a causal puzzle.

## 11.3 NPC and Faction Register

Important NPCs and factions should have consistent identifiers and records covering their goals, resources, relationships, information, and chapter appearances. Where an NPC or faction exists in both eras, document the connection without assuming that the modern version knows the full historical truth.

The register should distinguish objective facts from rumors, beliefs, lies, and competing interpretations. This distinction is essential to fair mystery design.

## 11.4 Mystery and Revelation Register

For each major mystery eventually developed, record:

- The actual underlying truth.
- What the DM must know from the outset.
- What the players initially encounter.
- What evidence can be found and where.
- Which interpretations are reasonable at each stage.
- What happens if clues are missed or misunderstood.
- Which later scenes depend on the revelation.
- Which conclusions are required for progression and which are optional.

This record prevents accidental contradictions and helps keep the mystery solvable without making it obvious.

## 11.5 Rules and Stat-Block Register

Every original mechanic and stat block must have a stable identifier, a source or design rationale, and a record of where it appears. If an entry is revised, check all encounters and appendices that use it.

Do not maintain multiple inconsistent copies of the same stat block in different chapters unless the differences are intentional and documented.

# 12. Production Workflow

The campaign should be developed in controlled stages. Writing chapters before the underlying requirements and dependencies are sufficiently stable risks extensive rewrites.

## Stage 1 — Requirements and Design Specification

Complete this document, resolve outstanding production decisions, and confirm the ruleset, manuscript format, page budget, density target, visual identity, and required campaign structure.

**Completion criterion:** every major production requirement is documented, and unresolved items are explicitly marked rather than silently assumed.

## Stage 2 — Campaign Architecture

Develop the story and plot separately from this specification. Establish the major events, character motivations, mysteries, timeline dependencies, and ending before drafting full chapters.

This stage is intentionally outside the scope of the current document. No specific villain, revelation, historical event, or finale outcome is prescribed here.

**Completion criterion:** the campaign has a coherent internal logic and its major dependencies can be tracked before detailed prose is written.

## Stage 3 — Chapter and Encounter Design

For each chapter, establish its playable structure, important locations, encounters, decisions, information flow, and transition requirements. Estimate the page types and column equivalents needed.

**Completion criterion:** the chapter can be described as a runnable adventure unit, not merely as a synopsis.

## Stage 4 — Manuscript Drafting

Draft in the established Markdown format, using consistent headings, notes, descriptive blocks, tables, and rules references. Maintain the canon, continuity, NPC, and mechanics registers as the manuscript evolves.

Drafting should proceed in manageable sections, with each section checked before later material depends on it.

**Completion criterion:** each section contains the information needed to run it, follows the agreed conventions, and has no unresolved placeholders that materially affect play.

## Stage 5 — Mechanical and Continuity Review

Check the manuscript against the 2024 rules, the established campaign canon, level progression, and cross-timeline consequences. Test whether encounters and procedures can be run without requiring the DM to invent essential rules or hidden assumptions.

**Completion criterion:** the manuscript is mechanically coherent and its established facts do not contradict one another.

## Stage 6 — Density and Layout Review

Compare the actual manuscript against the page budget and the reference density figures. Count publishable words, estimate column equivalents, identify pages that need maps or art, and adjust content where necessary.

Do not solve every pagination problem by shrinking type or removing useful explanations. Revise the content and layout according to their purpose.

**Completion criterion:** the book approaches its planned page count with appropriate variation in density and sufficient playable content.

## Stage 7 — Editorial and Usability Review

Review the manuscript from the perspective of a DM preparing and running a session. Check navigation, read-aloud separation, cross-references, table usability, encounter completeness, and the clarity of player-facing choices.

**Completion criterion:** the book is practical to run, consistent to read, and ready for final layout and proofreading.

# 13. Quality-Assurance Checklist

Every chapter must pass the following checks before it is considered complete.

### Structure
- [ ] Chapter purpose and level range are clear.
- [ ] Chapter has a playable beginning, sequence of situations, and outcomes.
- [ ] Chapter fits its allocated page budget or records a justified change.
- [ ] Transitions and cross-references are defined.

### Adventure Usability
- [ ] Locations and situations contain actionable DM information.
- [ ] Important NPCs have goals and usable behavior.
- [ ] Encounters account for likely approaches and consequences.
- [ ] Essential information has resilient discovery paths.
- [ ] The chapter can respond to plausible unexpected player actions.

### Dual-Timeline Continuity
- [ ] Facts are consistent with the canon ledger.
- [ ] Ancient actions with later consequences are recorded.
- [ ] Modern discoveries have traceable evidence.
- [ ] Player knowledge and character knowledge are handled fairly.

### Rules
- [ ] 2024 rules terminology is used consistently.
- [ ] Original mechanics define their triggers and outcomes.
- [ ] Encounter statistics and instructions are complete.
- [ ] Level advancement and resource assumptions are clear.

### Editorial and Formatting
- [ ] Heading hierarchy is correct.
- [ ] Homebrewery blocks are valid and used consistently.
- [ ] Tables are readable and have clear headings.
- [ ] Player-facing text is separated from DM-only information.
- [ ] Word count and column-equivalent estimates are recorded.

### Production
- [ ] Page types and visual requirements are budgeted.
- [ ] Art and maps have defined functions.
- [ ] Cross-references and identifiers are stable.
- [ ] No unresolved production placeholders remain.

# 14. Definition of a Successful Campaign Book

The completed manuscript will be considered successful when it meets all of the following conditions:

**Campaign completeness:** it delivers a coherent, full-length campaign with both timelines, a meaningful shared finale, and the established level progression.

**Player agency:** both character groups make consequential decisions, and neither timeline exists merely to explain or support the other.

**Rules integrity:** the campaign is written for the revised 2024 rules, with original mechanics fully specified and consistent with the chosen ruleset.

**DM usability:** the book supplies the procedures, encounter information, clues, NPC behavior, and consequences needed to run the adventure without writing substantial missing content.

**Continuity integrity:** established facts, historical events, character knowledge, and cross-timeline consequences are documented and internally consistent.

**Production density:** the manuscript is planned and measured using the reference figures of approximately 535 words per full-text-column equivalent and 678 words per text page on average, with suitable adjustments for maps, illustrations, tables, stat blocks, and mixed-content pages.

**Editorial quality:** the prose is clear, specific, evocative, and operational; headings, callouts, tables, and mechanical references are consistent.

**Visual coherence:** the layout and art direction support the mythic Art Deco identity without sacrificing legibility or usable content.

**Production readiness:** the manuscript can be converted into a polished campaign book without major structural rewrites or unresolved mechanical and continuity questions.

# 15. Decisions Intentionally Left Open

This document does not decide the campaign's plot. The following elements remain to be developed separately:

- The primary antagonist or antagonistic force.
- The underlying mystery and its final explanation.
- The major historical events the Netherese characters experience.
- The specific sites, factions, and NPCs involved.
- The sequence of revelations between the timelines.
- The precise mechanism through which the timelines intersect.
- The final threat, objectives, and possible endings.
- The specific branching decisions that determine the campaign's consequences.
- The final encounter roster, custom monsters, and original mechanical systems.
- The exact distribution of maps, illustrations, and page-level layouts.

Leaving these decisions open is deliberate. The production requirements establish what the campaign must achieve, how it must be written, and how its quality will be measured. The separate plot-development process will determine what happens.

---

## Governing Principle

*The Last Light of Netheril* must be written as a complete, playable, professionally structured campaign—not a synopsis expanded to fill 256 pages. Its length should emerge from the amount of useful adventure content, reference information, mechanical support, and visual material required to deliver the intended experience.

The manuscript's density should be measured, not guessed. Its continuity should be tracked, not remembered. Its rules should be specified, not inferred. Its two timelines should have independent agency, and their eventual intersection should be supported by the entire campaign's design.

This specification is the standard against which the future manuscript should be drafted, reviewed, and revised.
