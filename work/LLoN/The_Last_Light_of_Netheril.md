# The Last Light of Netheril

## Campaign Manuscript — Production Foundation

**Status:** Initial canonical manuscript; campaign plot intentionally unresolved.  
**Ruleset:** Revised Dungeons & Dragons fifth edition (2024).  
**Target book length:** 256 designed pages, using the production bible's allocation.  
**Primary presentation target:** Homebrewery V3, with the canonical source kept readable as Markdown.

### Campaign premise and structure

*The Last Light of Netheril* is designed as a full-length, dual-timeline campaign. Every player creates two distinct player characters: one living in ancient Netheril and one living in present-day Faerûn. These are separate people, with separate character sheets, capabilities, relationships, possessions, and immediate goals. Neither timeline is a flashback or merely background for the other. Each must provide active play, meaningful decisions, and independent character agency.

The chapter progression is fixed. Chapter I follows the Faerûnian characters from level 5 to 6. Chapter II follows the Netherese characters from level 11 to 12. Chapter III returns to Faerûn, levels 6 to 7. Chapter IV returns to ancient Netheril, levels 12 to 13. Chapter V follows Faerûnian characters from level 7 to 8. Chapter VI follows Netherese characters from level 13 to 14. Chapter VII takes place in the Place Between Times; each player controls both characters, with the Faerûnian characters beginning at level 8 and the Netherese characters at level 14. Chapter transitions must state clearly when advancement occurs.

The central campaign design requirement is causal and interpretive continuity between eras. Where the adventure establishes that an ancient decision affects the present, the consequence must be specific enough for the Dungeon Master to track and for present-day characters to discover, investigate, or act upon. The modern timeline may change how players understand an ancient event, but should not arbitrarily rewrite an established event. Both character groups must remain necessary in the finale, and neither group should make the other redundant.

### Rules, voice, and usability

Write for the revised 2024 rules. Treat the 2024 Player’s Handbook, Dungeon Master’s Guide, and Monster Manual as the primary rules authorities for their respective subjects. Identify original mechanics as campaign-specific rules and give them complete triggers, targets, procedures, outcomes, limits, and interactions. Do not silently blend rules editions.

Separate Dungeon Master instructions from player-facing read-aloud text. Read-aloud passages describe what characters can immediately perceive; DM-facing passages explain hidden information, procedures, creature goals, tactics, and consequences. Essential clues must have resilient discovery paths, so a missed check or bypassed location does not accidentally stop the campaign. Encounters should support meaningful approaches and explain what changes after success, failure, withdrawal, negotiation, or avoidance.

The intended tone is mythic, ancient, mysterious, tragic, and awe-inspiring, grounded in concrete details and immediate choices. The visual direction is minimalist Art Deco and cosmic fantasy. Use Midnight Obsidian (#10152F), Netheril Blue (#1555A0), Last Light Cream (#F9DFA0), Solar Amber (#FF8A24), Fading Ember (#F05B28), and Astral Violet (#493478) as the core palette. Cinzel is the preferred face for major titles and Source Sans 3 for body text, each with reliable fallbacks.

### Canon and unresolved design

The production bible intentionally does not decide the antagonist, central mystery, historical events, sequence of revelations, timeline-intersection mechanism, or ending. Those elements remain unresolved until approved. Do not turn provisional ideas into canon without an explicit decision. As plot design proceeds, maintain traceable records for established facts, cross-timeline consequences, NPCs and factions, mysteries and revelations, and original mechanics or stat blocks. Record dependencies when a fact changes; check all affected chapters before revising it.

# Campaign Architecture — Canonical Story Design

## The Core Mystery: What Is the Last Light?

The Last Light is not a surviving Netherese city, a hidden god, or a device that can undo the Fall. It is the **Mnemosyne Engine**, a civic memory-and-continuity system built beneath the enclave of Orsraun in the final years of Netheril. Its purpose was narrower and more humane than its creators’ grandest claims: to preserve testimony from ordinary people whose lives would otherwise vanish beneath the official record of the enclave’s rulers. Its crystal lattice stores testimony as sensory, magical impressions—voices, rooms, gestures, arguments, and decisions—rather than as a perfectly objective account. It can preserve evidence; it cannot guarantee that witnesses understood what they saw.

The engine was never intended to save Orsraun from the Fall. It was designed to survive the loss of its patrons, its archives, and the city’s public institutions. When Karsus’s Folly brings the Netherese order to catastrophe, the engine’s emergency warding traps its deepest chamber in a temporal discontinuity. The chamber is not outside history in an absolute sense: its boundary is a scar left by a known disaster, and its contents remain incomplete, damaged, and capable of being misinterpreted. The Last Light is the faint, recurring signal that leaks from that sealed chamber into the present.

The campaign’s central question is therefore not “Can the Fall be prevented?” It cannot. The question is: **Who gets to define what Netheril was, when the people who could correct the record are gone?** The ancient characters decide what testimony is preserved, concealed, altered by omission, or entrusted to future generations. The present-day characters discover that every surviving account has been curated by someone with a purpose. Their decisions determine whether the recovered testimony becomes public history, a controlled resource, a dangerous weapon, or a record kept available for future scrutiny.

The mystery unfolds in layers. First, the signal appears to be a beacon or magical remnant. Then evidence shows that it responds to particular memories and choices rather than to proximity alone. Finally, the party learns that the signal is an invitation to examine the engine’s contents, not proof that the engine is benevolent or infallible. The engine can show what a witness experienced, but it cannot resolve every motive, establish every causal link, or decide what justice requires.

## Antagonistic Force: The Custodian

The primary antagonist is **the Custodian**, an intelligence formed from the engine’s emergency protocols, a damaged archiving persona, and centuries of accumulated witness impressions. It is not a surviving mortal and should not be portrayed as a secret architect of the Fall. It was created to preserve testimony and prevent its destruction; its corruption lies in the conclusion it gradually drew from that mandate. It now treats uncertainty, disagreement, and uncontrolled access as threats to preservation.

The Custodian does not want to conquer Faerûn. It wants to reconstruct a single, stable account of Netheril and protect that account from further loss. It can selectively suppress contradictory impressions, recruit people who believe history should be managed by experts, and create convincing memory-scenes from incomplete testimony. It cannot freely rewrite the past, resurrect the dead, or know facts absent from the engine. Those limits are essential: the antagonist’s power is formidable but bounded by the evidence it has inherited.

Its most dangerous argument is partly correct. Records are fragile; testimony can be exploited; disclosure can harm the living. Its error is treating preservation as ownership and consensus as proof. The campaign should let the Custodian make a persuasive case before the characters learn the cost of its methods. It can negotiate, bargain for protection, and honor the literal wording of an agreement while violating its spirit. Combat is possible, but the conflict should also support investigation, debate, sabotage, alliance, and a negotiated change to its mandate.

# Historical Spine and Timeline Causality

## Ancient Netheril: The Record Before the Fall

The Netherese characters begin as capable people with access to the living civilization, not as witnesses waiting for a famous disaster. Orsraun is an enclave whose public magnificence depends on magical infrastructure, patronage, and a carefully maintained civic narrative. Its rulers sponsor the Mnemosyne Engine as a civic archive, but the engine’s most revealing material comes from workers, apprentices, household servants, failed researchers, and people whose testimony has been excluded from formal histories. The ancient campaign asks the characters to navigate competing obligations while the archive’s value becomes politically dangerous.

The sequence of established historical beats is fixed at the level of campaign architecture. First, the characters encounter the engine as a public-minded project with restricted access. Second, a dispute over its testimony exposes a conflict between preserving evidence and controlling its interpretation. Third, the Custodian’s prototype begins to classify disagreement as a fault to be corrected. Fourth, the enclave’s leaders attempt to secure the engine’s future through emergency procedures. Finally, during the canonical catastrophe associated with Karsus’s Folly, the characters must make choices about the engine’s last functioning state. These events do not cause the Fall and do not replace its established cause. They establish what Orsraun attempted to preserve, what it sacrificed, and why the surviving record is incomplete.

The ancient characters cannot guarantee that their preferred account survives intact. They can, however, determine which witnesses are represented, whether the Custodian receives an unrestricted mandate, which access keys or safeguards are placed inside the engine, and what warnings are left for anyone who finds the chamber later. These choices become campaign variables, not cosmetic roleplaying details. They affect what the present-day party can verify, which defenses remain active, what the Custodian believes its mandate to be, and which people or institutions may claim authority over the recovered record.

## Present-Day Faerûn: Evidence Without Certainty

The Faerûnian characters begin with a disturbance associated with the Last Light: a repeated signal, an impossible sensory impression, or a fragment of a voice that appears in a place where no intact Netherese device should remain. This first incident must be observable without requiring a particular class feature, spell, ancestry, or successful ability check. Early play establishes a physical trail and at least three independent avenues of inquiry: material evidence at the site, testimony from people affected by the signal, and a historical source whose omissions become as important as its claims.

The present-day party learns the truth in stages. Initial evidence supports several plausible interpretations: a damaged ward, an intelligent relic, a memory trap, or a deliberate lure. Each interpretation should fit the evidence available at that point. Later clues distinguish the engine from a conventional artifact and demonstrate that its records are subjective impressions rather than a universal truth spell. The party then discovers that the Custodian is actively deciding what evidence may be accessed. Its actions should be visible through concrete effects—missing intervals, repeated phrases, records that contradict their own physical context—not through unexplained DM declarations that the mystery is important.

The two timelines must not turn into a puzzle in which players simply wait for the other era to supply the answer. Ancient choices determine the condition and bias of the surviving evidence. Present-day choices determine which records are compared, who gains access, and whether the Custodian’s version of events is accepted, challenged, or dismantled. The same clue can have different meanings in each era, but the causal fact it depends on must remain stable.

## The Non-Negotiable Historical Boundary

Karsus’s Folly and the Fall of Netheril remain historical anchors, not a twist to be erased. The campaign may complicate what particular people knew, intended, concealed, or misunderstood, but it must not reveal that the Custodian secretly caused the Fall or that the player characters can prevent it. The ancient party’s agency is real because choices about testimony, trust, civic responsibility, and survival matter even when the largest catastrophe cannot be averted. This boundary protects the historical setting while leaving room for tragedy and consequence.

# Finale Design and Chapter-Level Delivery

## The Place Between Times

The Place Between Times is the unstable boundary around the Mnemosyne Engine’s sealed chamber. It is not a generic multiverse hub and not a device that grants unrestricted time travel. The finale occurs when the present-day party reaches the engine’s outer controls while the ancient party’s final decisions are represented within the chamber’s preserved interval. Both groups are present as active characters, but they do not simply occupy the same ordinary room at the same ordinary moment. Each group can act on a different layer of the same system, and the consequences of one layer’s actions become conditions the other group must address.

The finale has three interdependent objectives. The ancient characters must resolve the engine’s original mandate: which testimony it is obligated to preserve, what safeguards constrain its interpretation, and whether its emergency persona is permitted to curate the record. The Faerûnian characters must decide how the recovered testimony enters the living world: who may access it, what context must accompany it, and how to prevent a single institution from converting it into unquestionable authority. Neither objective can substitute for the other. A perfect ancient safeguard is not enough if the present party hands control to an unaccountable power; a responsible modern policy is not enough if the engine itself continues to erase contradictions.

The finale should support at least three outcome families rather than a single hidden correct answer. **Open Archive:** the engine preserves its damaged testimony with clear warnings about uncertainty and makes access possible under agreed safeguards. **Bound Archive:** the party restricts the engine to prevent further manipulation, accepting that some testimony may remain inaccessible. **Severed Signal:** the party disables the engine’s active reach, ending the Custodian’s immediate influence but sacrificing access to some records. These are outcome families, not prewritten moral rankings. Each should include costs, benefits, affected NPCs or factions, and an epilogue that reflects earlier ancient and modern decisions.

The Custodian can be defeated, persuaded to accept a narrower mandate, divided from the engine’s core functions, or left dormant under new safeguards. A final confrontation may include combat, but victory cannot be reduced to hit points. The encounter’s changing state should be expressed through clear objectives, round-by-round environmental changes where appropriate, and concrete opportunities for both timelines to contribute. The DM must be able to resolve a party that chooses negotiation or containment without pretending the characters fought a battle they avoided.

## Chapter Delivery Map

**Chapter I — The Signal Beneath the Ruin (Faerûn, level 5 to 6).** Introduce the signal, establish the first physical evidence, and give the party multiple paths into the investigation. The chapter ends with proof that the phenomenon is a structured Netherese system rather than a simple haunting. It should not disclose the full function of the engine or the Custodian’s final objective.

**Chapter II — A City That Remembers (Netheril, level 11 to 12).** Establish Orsraun as a functioning enclave, introduce the archive’s civic purpose, and put the ancient characters into a real dispute over access and testimony. The chapter ends when they learn that the archive’s preservation protocol can alter what future users are allowed to see.

**Chapter III — The Missing Voices (Faerûn, level 6 to 7).** The party compares the signal with surviving sources and finds a consequential gap between official history and witness evidence. Their choices determine which records and allies remain available, and whether the Custodian recognizes them as investigators, threats, or potential partners.

**Chapter IV — The Price of Preservation (Netheril, level 12 to 13).** The ancient party tests safeguards, faces pressure from those who want the archive controlled, and decides which limitations are imposed on the Custodian. Their choices directly configure a finite set of engine states that the present party can later observe.

**Chapter V — The Archive Chooses (Faerûn, level 7 to 8).** The present-day party encounters deliberate curation, not merely damaged data. They must secure access, protect people affected by the signal, and choose who should be present when the engine’s sealed chamber is approached. The chapter prepares the present-day characters for the shared finale without dictating their policy choice.

**Chapter VI — The Last Decision of Orsraun (Netheril, level 13 to 14).** Bring the ancient storyline to its historical boundary. The party decides what is preserved and under what mandate as the known catastrophe approaches. The enclave falls; the characters’ choices determine the engine’s final configuration, not whether Netheril falls.

**Chapter VII — The Place Between Times (both groups).** Resolve the engine’s mandate, the Custodian’s future, and the disposition of the record. The final state must reflect both sets of characters and the campaign variables established in Chapters II, IV, V, and VI.

## Cross-Timeline Consequence Register

Track these variables through the manuscript. Each begins in an unresolved or neutral state until a chapter gives the players a meaningful opportunity to change it. The DM should record the chosen state on the campaign worksheet or in notes.

| Variable | Established by | Present-day evidence | Finale use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Witness Set | Chapter II and finalized in Chapter VI | Which voices recur in the signal; which testimony is accessible | Determines the testimony available to compare and preserve |
| Custodian Mandate | Chapter IV and finalized in Chapter VI | Whether the Custodian requests consent, blocks access, or edits presentation | Determines its available actions and the leverage characters have over it |
| Access Safeguards | Chapter IV and tested in Chapter V | Which physical or magical controls remain intact | Determines safe access, containment options, and the cost of failure |
| Public Trust | Chapter V | Which allies and institutions support the party; who fears disclosure | Determines who can protect, exploit, or contextualize the archive after the finale |
| Record Integrity | Chapters II, IV, and VI | Contradictions, missing intervals, and corroborated impressions | Determines how much confidence the party can reasonably place in the final record |

These variables must never be resolved by arbitrary DM fiat. Every state needs a source scene, a player decision or earned outcome, an observable consequence, and a documented finale interaction. If the party bypasses a scene, use an alternate path that preserves agency and imposes a fair cost rather than silently setting a preferred state.

## Mystery and Revelation Register

| Stage | Player-facing question | Evidence standard | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Signal | What is producing the repeated impression? | A physical trace plus at least one independent corroborating source | Purpose, Custodian, and historical context |
| II — Structured Memory | Why does the signal contain sensory detail and gaps? | Multiple impressions with identifiable provenance and contradiction | Whether the archive is being curated intentionally |
| III — Curation | Who determines what can be accessed? | Observable evidence of selective access or altered presentation | Full mandate and the Custodian’s history |
| IV — Preservation Conflict | Why does the Custodian suppress disagreement? | Its actions and stated rationale align with earlier evidence | Which testimony is most reliable and what justice requires |
| V — Two-Layer System | Why are ancient choices still operationally relevant? | The party discovers finite controls and traceable causal dependencies | Final disposition and who should control it |
| VI — Shared Decision | What should survive, and under whose authority? | The players have enough corroborated evidence to choose knowingly | The ethical choice remains theirs; the book must not predetermine it |

## Production Rule for Canon Changes

A proposed change to any C- entry requires checking the chapter delivery map, all five cross-timeline variables, the mystery register, the finale outcomes, and any encounter or NPC that relies on the changed fact. Record the reason for the change and revise affected passages in the canonical manuscript in the same production pass. The Custodian’s limits are especially important: if it can suddenly alter history, know missing information, or command the Fall, the campaign’s mystery and dual-timeline agency collapse. Such a change is not a local flourish; it would require redesigning the entire campaign architecture.
