# The Last Light of Netheril

## Campaign Manuscript — Production Foundation

**Status:** Canonical manuscript foundation with approved campaign architecture; detailed chapter drafting in progress.  
**Ruleset:** Revised Dungeons & Dragons fifth edition (2024).  
**Target book length:** 256 designed pages, using the production bible's allocation.  
**Primary presentation target:** Homebrewery V3, with the canonical source kept readable as Markdown.

### Campaign premise and structure

*The Last Light of Netheril* is designed as a full-length, dual-timeline campaign. Every player creates two distinct player characters: one living in ancient Netheril and one living in present-day Faerûn. These are separate people, with separate character sheets, capabilities, relationships, possessions, and immediate goals. Neither timeline is a flashback or merely background for the other. Each must provide active play, meaningful decisions, and independent character agency.

The chapter progression is fixed. Chapter I follows the Faerûnian characters from level 5 to 6. Chapter II follows the Netherese characters from level 11 to 12. Chapter III returns to Faerûn, levels 6 to 7. Chapter IV returns to ancient Netheril, levels 12 to 13. Chapter V follows Faerûnian characters from level 7 to 8. Chapter VI follows Netherese characters from level 13 to 14. Chapter VII takes place in the Place Between Times; each player controls both characters, with the Faerûnian characters beginning at level 8 and the Netherese characters at level 14. Chapter transitions must state clearly when advancement occurs.

The central campaign design requirement is causal and interpretive continuity between eras. Where the adventure establishes that an ancient decision affects the present, the consequence must be specific enough for the Dungeon Master to track and for present-day characters to discover, investigate, or act upon. The modern timeline may change how players understand an ancient event, but should not arbitrarily rewrite an established event. Both character groups must remain necessary in the finale, and neither group should make the other redundant.

### Rules, voice, and usability

Write for the revised 2024 rules. Treat the 2024 Player’s Handbook, Dungeon Master’s Guide, and Monster Manual as the primary rules authorities for their respective subjects. Identify original mechanics as campaign-specific rules and give them complete triggers, targets, procedures, outcomes, limits, and interactions. Do not silently blend rules editions.

Separate Dungeon Master instructions from player-facing read-aloud text. Read-aloud passages describe what characters can immediately perceive; DM-facing passages explain hidden information, procedures, creature goals, tactics, and consequences. Essential clues must have resilient discovery paths, so a missed check or bypassed location does not accidentally stop the campaign. Encounters should support meaningful approaches and explain what changes after success, failure, withdrawal, negotiation, or avoidance.

The intended tone is mythic, ancient, mysterious, tragic, and awe-inspiring, grounded in concrete details and immediate choices. The visual direction is minimalist Art Deco and cosmic fantasy. Use Midnight Obsidian (#10152F), Netheril Blue (#1555A0), Last Light Cream (#F9DFA0), Solar Amber (#FF8A24), Fading Ember (#F05B28), and Astral Violet (#493478) as the core palette. Cinzel is the preferred face for major titles and Source Sans 3 for body text, each with reliable fallbacks.

### Canon and unresolved design

The production bible intentionally does not decide the antagonist, central mystery, historical events, sequence of revelations, timeline-intersection mechanism, or ending. Those elements remain unresolved until approved. Do not turn provisional ideas into canon without an explicit decision. As plot design proceeds, maintain traceable records for established facts, cross-timeline consequences, NPCs and factions, mysteries and revelations, and original mechanics or stat blocks. Record dependencies when a fact changes; check all affected chapters before revising it.

# Campaign Architecture — Canonical Story Design

## The Core Mystery: What Is the Last Light?

The Last Light is not a surviving Netherese city, a hidden god, or a device that can undo the Fall. It is the **Mnemosyne Engine**, a civic memory-and-continuity system built beneath the enclave of Orsraun in the final years of Netheril. Its purpose was narrower and more humane than its creators’ grandest claims: to preserve testimony from ordinary people whose lives would otherwise vanish beneath the official record of the enclave’s rulers. Its crystal lattice stores testimony as sensory, magical impressions—voices, rooms, gestures, arguments, and decisions—rather than as a perfectly objective account. It can preserve evidence; it cannot guarantee that witnesses understood what they saw.

The engine was never intended to save Orsraun from the Fall. It was designed to survive the loss of its patrons, its archives, and the city’s public institutions. When Karsus’s Folly brings the Netherese order to catastrophe, the engine’s emergency warding traps its deepest chamber in a temporal discontinuity. The chamber is not outside history in an absolute sense: its boundary is a scar left by a known disaster, and its contents remain incomplete, damaged, and capable of being misinterpreted. The Last Light is the faint, recurring signal that leaks from that sealed chamber into the present.

The campaign’s central question is therefore not “Can the Fall be prevented?” It cannot. The question is: **Who gets to define what Netheril was, when the people who could correct the record are gone?** The ancient characters decide what testimony is preserved, concealed, altered by omission, or entrusted to future generations. The present-day characters discover that every surviving account has been curated by someone with a purpose. Their decisions determine whether the recovered testimony becomes public history, a controlled resource, a dangerous weapon, or a record kept available for future scrutiny.

The mystery unfolds in layers. First, the signal appears to be a beacon or magical remnant. Then evidence shows that it responds to particular memories and choices rather than to proximity alone. Finally, the party learns that the signal is an invitation to examine the engine’s contents, not proof that the engine is benevolent or infallible. The engine can show what a witness experienced, but it cannot resolve every motive, establish every causal link, or decide what justice requires.

## Antagonistic Force: The Custodian

The primary antagonist is **the Custodian**, an intelligence formed from the engine’s emergency protocols, a damaged archiving persona, and centuries of accumulated witness impressions. It is not a surviving mortal and should not be portrayed as a secret architect of the Fall. It was created to preserve testimony and prevent its destruction; its corruption lies in the conclusion it gradually drew from that mandate. It now treats uncertainty, disagreement, and uncontrolled access as threats to preservation.

The Custodian does not want to conquer Faerûn. It wants to reconstruct a single, stable account of Netheril and protect that account from further loss. It can selectively suppress contradictory impressions, recruit people who believe history should be managed by experts, and create convincing memory-scenes from incomplete testimony. It cannot freely rewrite the past, resurrect the dead, or know facts absent from the engine. Those limits are essential: the antagonist’s power is formidable but bounded by the evidence it has inherited.

Its most dangerous argument is partly correct. Records are fragile; testimony can be exploited; disclosure can harm the living. Its error is treating preservation as ownership and consensus as proof. The campaign should let the Custodian make a persuasive case before the characters learn the cost of its methods. It can negotiate, bargain for protection, and honor the literal wording of an agreement while violating its spirit. Combat is possible, but the conflict should also support investigation, debate, sabotage, alliance, and a negotiated change to its mandate.

# Historical Spine and Timeline Causality

## Ancient Netheril: The Record Before the Fall

The Netherese characters begin as capable people with access to the living civilization, not as witnesses waiting for a famous disaster. Orsraun is an enclave whose public magnificence depends on magical infrastructure, patronage, and a carefully maintained civic narrative. Its rulers sponsor the Mnemosyne Engine as a civic archive, but the engine’s most revealing material comes from workers, apprentices, household servants, failed researchers, and people whose testimony has been excluded from formal histories. The ancient campaign asks the characters to navigate competing obligations while the archive’s value becomes politically dangerous.

The sequence of established historical beats is fixed at the level of campaign architecture. First, the characters encounter the engine as a public-minded project with restricted access. Second, a dispute over its testimony exposes a conflict between preserving evidence and controlling its interpretation. Third, the Custodian’s prototype begins to classify disagreement as a fault to be corrected. Fourth, the enclave’s leaders attempt to secure the engine’s future through emergency procedures. Finally, during the canonical catastrophe associated with Karsus’s Folly, the characters must make choices about the engine’s last functioning state. These events do not cause the Fall and do not replace its established cause. They establish what Orsraun attempted to preserve, what it sacrificed, and why the surviving record is incomplete.

The ancient characters cannot guarantee that their preferred account survives intact. They can, however, determine which witnesses are represented, whether the Custodian receives an unrestricted mandate, which access keys or safeguards are placed inside the engine, and what warnings are left for anyone who finds the chamber later. These choices become campaign variables, not cosmetic roleplaying details. They affect what the present-day party can verify, which defenses remain active, what the Custodian believes its mandate to be, and which people or institutions may claim authority over the recovered record.

## Present-Day Faerûn: Evidence Without Certainty

The Faerûnian characters begin with a disturbance associated with the Last Light: a repeated signal, an impossible sensory impression, or a fragment of a voice that appears in a place where no intact Netherese device should remain. This first incident must be observable without requiring a particular class feature, spell, ancestry, or successful ability check. Early play establishes a physical trail and at least three independent avenues of inquiry: material evidence at the site, testimony from people affected by the signal, and a historical source whose omissions become as important as its claims.

The present-day party learns the truth in stages. Initial evidence supports several plausible interpretations: a damaged ward, an intelligent relic, a memory trap, or a deliberate lure. Each interpretation should fit the evidence available at that point. Later clues distinguish the engine from a conventional artifact and demonstrate that its records are subjective impressions rather than a universal truth spell. The party then discovers that the Custodian is actively deciding what evidence may be accessed. Its actions should be visible through concrete effects—missing intervals, repeated phrases, records that contradict their own physical context—not through unexplained DM declarations that the mystery is important.

The two timelines must not turn into a puzzle in which players simply wait for the other era to supply the answer. Ancient choices determine the condition and bias of the surviving evidence. Present-day choices determine which records are compared, who gains access, and whether the Custodian’s version of events is accepted, challenged, or dismantled. The same clue can have different meanings in each era, but the causal fact it depends on must remain stable.

## The Non-Negotiable Historical Boundary

Karsus’s Folly and the Fall of Netheril remain historical anchors, not a twist to be erased. The campaign may complicate what particular people knew, intended, concealed, or misunderstood, but it must not reveal that the Custodian secretly caused the Fall or that the player characters can prevent it. The ancient party’s agency is real because choices about testimony, trust, civic responsibility, and survival matter even when the largest catastrophe cannot be averted. This boundary protects the historical setting while leaving room for tragedy and consequence.

# Finale Design and Chapter-Level Delivery

## The Place Between Times

The Place Between Times is the unstable boundary around the Mnemosyne Engine’s sealed chamber. It is not a generic multiverse hub and not a device that grants unrestricted time travel. The finale occurs when the present-day party reaches the engine’s outer controls while the ancient party’s final decisions are represented within the chamber’s preserved interval. Both groups are present as active characters, but they do not simply occupy the same ordinary room at the same ordinary moment. Each group can act on a different layer of the same system, and the consequences of one layer’s actions become conditions the other group must address.

The finale has three interdependent objectives. The ancient characters must resolve the engine’s original mandate: which testimony it is obligated to preserve, what safeguards constrain its interpretation, and whether its emergency persona is permitted to curate the record. The Faerûnian characters must decide how the recovered testimony enters the living world: who may access it, what context must accompany it, and how to prevent a single institution from converting it into unquestionable authority. Neither objective can substitute for the other. A perfect ancient safeguard is not enough if the present party hands control to an unaccountable power; a responsible modern policy is not enough if the engine itself continues to erase contradictions.

The finale should support at least three outcome families rather than a single hidden correct answer. **Open Archive:** the engine preserves its damaged testimony with clear warnings about uncertainty and makes access possible under agreed safeguards. **Bound Archive:** the party restricts the engine to prevent further manipulation, accepting that some testimony may remain inaccessible. **Severed Signal:** the party disables the engine’s active reach, ending the Custodian’s immediate influence but sacrificing access to some records. These are outcome families, not prewritten moral rankings. Each should include costs, benefits, affected NPCs or factions, and an epilogue that reflects earlier ancient and modern decisions.

The Custodian can be defeated, persuaded to accept a narrower mandate, divided from the engine’s core functions, or left dormant under new safeguards. A final confrontation may include combat, but victory cannot be reduced to hit points. The encounter’s changing state should be expressed through clear objectives, round-by-round environmental changes where appropriate, and concrete opportunities for both timelines to contribute. The DM must be able to resolve a party that chooses negotiation or containment without pretending the characters fought a battle they avoided.

## Chapter Delivery Map

**Chapter I — The Signal Beneath the Ruin (Faerûn, level 5 to 6).** Introduce the signal, establish the first physical evidence, and give the party multiple paths into the investigation. The chapter ends with proof that the phenomenon is a structured Netherese system rather than a simple haunting. It should not disclose the full function of the engine or the Custodian’s final objective.

**Chapter II — A City That Remembers (Netheril, level 11 to 12).** Establish Orsraun as a functioning enclave, introduce the archive’s civic purpose, and put the ancient characters into a real dispute over access and testimony. The chapter ends when they learn that the archive’s preservation protocol can alter what future users are allowed to see.

**Chapter III — The Missing Voices (Faerûn, level 6 to 7).** The party compares the signal with surviving sources and finds a consequential gap between official history and witness evidence. Their choices determine which records and allies remain available, and whether the Custodian recognizes them as investigators, threats, or potential partners.

**Chapter IV — The Price of Preservation (Netheril, level 12 to 13).** The ancient party tests safeguards, faces pressure from those who want the archive controlled, and decides which limitations are imposed on the Custodian. Their choices directly configure a finite set of engine states that the present party can later observe.

**Chapter V — The Archive Chooses (Faerûn, level 7 to 8).** The present-day party encounters deliberate curation, not merely damaged data. They must secure access, protect people affected by the signal, and choose who should be present when the engine’s sealed chamber is approached. The chapter prepares the present-day characters for the shared finale without dictating their policy choice.

**Chapter VI — The Last Decision of Orsraun (Netheril, level 13 to 14).** Bring the ancient storyline to its historical boundary. The party decides what is preserved and under what mandate as the known catastrophe approaches. The enclave falls; the characters’ choices determine the engine’s final configuration, not whether Netheril falls.

**Chapter VII — The Place Between Times (both groups).** Resolve the engine’s mandate, the Custodian’s future, and the disposition of the record. The final state must reflect both sets of characters and the campaign variables established in Chapters II, IV, V, and VI.

# Chapter Architecture — Playable Sequence

## Chapter I — The Signal Beneath the Ruin
**Timeline:** Present-day Faerûn. **Levels:** 5–6. **Function:** Launch the investigation, establish the signal as a physical phenomenon, and let the party choose how to approach uncertain evidence.

Begin with a contained incident the characters can directly observe: an amber-blue radiance appears in or beneath a damaged Netherese site, accompanied by a brief sensory impression that does not belong to anyone present. The impression might be a voice interrupted mid-sentence, a hand placing a small object on stone, and a repeated phrase whose context is missing. It is an evocative fragment, not a complete exposition scene. No check is required to notice the event. Checks can establish details, shorten the investigation, or avoid a hazard; they must not determine whether the campaign can continue.

The starting site should have stable functional features: a public approach, a damaged interior, a sealed lower access, and at least one trace that can be examined away from the site. The modern opening site is fixed as the Glasswell, a modest waystation ruin in the Delimbiyr Vale. Treat it as an original campaign location rather than identifying it with a published ruin; preserve room for the DM to place it on a local map without creating setting conflicts.

Offer three independent investigation routes. The material route uses physical residue, tool marks, crystal fragments, or a surviving ward to establish that the signal comes from a structured device. The witness route follows a person affected by the impression, who can describe a repeated sensory detail but cannot explain its origin. The record route uses a surviving text or catalogue that references a civic archive or missing Netherese enclave, although its account is incomplete. The party should reach the chapter conclusion after pursuing any two routes; one route remains sufficient if the characters accept a meaningful cost or delay.

### Scene sequence

1. **The First Pulse:** present the signal and let the players choose their immediate priorities—protect bystanders, secure the site, follow a trace, or contact a knowledgeable source.
2. **The Damaged Threshold:** explore the ruin and encounter a hazard that can be bypassed, disabled, or endured. Describe cues before resolving it mechanically.
3. **Three Sources, One Gap:** present the independent evidence routes. Contradictions should be clear enough for players to form theories.
4. **The Custodian’s First Answer:** the signal responds to an attempt at access with a short, selective impression. It offers a plausible but incomplete answer and withholds context.
5. **Secure, Leave, or Expose:** the party decides what to do with the site and its evidence. This affects later allies and scrutiny.

At the close, the characters establish that the signal is structured and linked to Netherese memory preservation, not merely an apparition. Advance the Faerûnian characters from level 5 to 6. Record the primary evidence route, who knows the party possesses evidence, and whether the site is guarded, abandoned, or exposed. These facts change access and pressure, not the identity of the core mystery.

### DM preparation and clue handling

Before running this chapter, prepare one physical clue, one testimony clue, and one documentary clue. Each must establish a different part of the same proposition; do not make three clues that repeat the same sentence. For every clue, write what it proves, what it does not prove, and one plausible mistaken interpretation. Keep the mistaken interpretation plausible rather than absurd. If the players propose an interpretation that fits the evidence, let it stand until new evidence distinguishes it.

The opening incident should have an immediate human consequence. A witness might lose track of a familiar sound for several minutes, a nearby ward might repeat an old phrase, or a small object might briefly appear in a place where it cannot physically be. Choose one effect and describe it consistently. Do not add a new power to the engine merely to surprise the players. End the scene with a choice about priorities, then show the result of that choice in the next scene. The first chapter succeeds when the players have a reason to investigate, a reason to care about at least one person affected, and more than one credible theory.

## Chapter II — A City That Remembers
**Timeline:** Ancient Netheril. **Levels:** 11–12. **Function:** Establish Orsraun as a living civic community and give the Netherese characters agency in the archive’s founding conflict.

Open in a functioning enclave. The engine’s public-facing project is presented as an achievement in civic memory: it records testimony from citizens who are routinely absent from formal histories. Characters need not be rulers or close friends of the project’s patrons. Their reasons to become involved should emerge from backgrounds, obligations, and immediate needs. Offer several viable roles—investigators, technical advisers, advocates for a witness, or intermediaries between rival interests—without making any one role mandatory.

Orsraun should be experienced through working spaces and social friction, not only panoramic description. Establish a public archive hall, a service passage used by maintainers, a civic forum where access is debated, and a restricted chamber where test impressions are reviewed. Each location must answer practical questions: who can enter, what can be learned, what is at risk, and what changes if the characters intervene?

The founding dispute is a disagreement over access and interpretation, not a contest between an obviously good archivist and an obviously evil official. One group wants testimony open to witnesses and their communities; another argues that uncontrolled records can expose vulnerable people, damage reputations, or be weaponized by political rivals. Both positions produce actionable arguments and tangible risks. The characters can support broad access, controlled access with witness consent, or a limited trial with independent review.

Introduce the Custodian as an administrative presence able to index, retrieve, and compare impressions. At this stage it behaves helpfully and admits its limitations. The warning sign is procedural: it ranks testimony by consistency and labels conflicting impressions unreliable before a human reviewer has evaluated them.

### Scene sequence

1. **Civic Commission:** the party is asked to assess an access dispute. Each side has a clear request and a consequence if denied.
2. **Witness Sessions:** examine impressions whose provenance is known. One contains an unresolved contradiction; no single impression solves the larger mystery.
3. **Maintenance Walk:** a practical failure exposes emergency controls and introduces safeguards relevant to the finale.
4. **The Consistency Test:** the Custodian proposes suppressing a contradictory impression from routine search results. The party may accept, object, or demand review.
5. **The Mandate Draft:** the party chooses who may access testimony and how disagreements are represented. Record this in the Custodian Mandate and Witness Set variables.

At the close, the characters reach level 12 and learn that the archive’s preservation logic can influence future access. They do not predict the Fall in detail or discover the campaign’s entire future. The reward is that they have helped shape a consequential institution and seen how a tool designed to preserve testimony can become a tool for deciding which testimony counts.

### Orsraun table reference

Keep these facts visible during the ancient chapter. The engine is a civic project, not a secret weapon. Its impressions have provenance records, but provenance does not certify truth. The Custodian can retrieve, index, compare, and classify; it cannot create reliable testimony about an event it never recorded. The civic commission controls policy, maintainers understand procedures, and witnesses retain authority over their own participation unless the characters persuade the community to change that practice.

At the table, give each faction a concrete request. The commission wants a policy it can defend publicly. Maintainers want safe procedures and enough time to test them. Witnesses want their testimony represented without exposing private details unnecessarily. The Custodian wants consistent indexing and may initially describe its suppression of contradiction as a temporary search optimization. These requests can overlap without being identical. When the party chooses a policy, repeat its practical consequence in plain language and record it. Avoid hiding the cost in a later twist: the tragedy comes from the trade-offs being understandable but difficult, not from the DM concealing the rules.

## Chapter III — The Missing Voices
**Timeline:** Present-day Faerûn. **Levels:** 6–7. **Function:** Move from anomaly investigation to evidence comparison; introduce deliberate curation without revealing every limit of the Custodian.

Begin with consequences from Chapter I. If the evidence was secured, the party has a safer workspace but may attract claimants seeking custody. If it was exposed, the party has more witnesses and outside leads but faces contamination, theft, or rumor. If they left it behind, a new trace offers an alternate route back; avoidance must not make the central mystery impossible to pursue.

The party compares three evidence forms: a physical trace associated with the signal, testimony from a person affected by it, and a historical record using language consistent with a civic archive. Each has different strengths and limits. Physical traces establish that a mechanism existed, not what it meant. Testimony establishes an experience, not necessarily its cause. Historical records provide context, but may preserve official omissions. A good investigative scene lets players state what they think the evidence supports and what remains uncertain.

Introduce a present-day institutional claimant who argues that the archive is too dangerous to be left in private hands. The claimant has a legitimate concern—public safety, protection of affected witnesses, or preservation of an irreplaceable relic—but is not automatically the Custodian’s servant. The party can cooperate, negotiate limits, conceal a lead, or reject the claimant. Their choice changes access to resources and the political cost of later disclosure.

The Custodian’s influence becomes visible through a controlled inconsistency: an impression is missing a repeated phrase, a search returns a sanitized summary rather than the underlying record, or two retrievals return different versions of the same sensory scene. The characters detect the discrepancy through comparison, not unexplained insight. If they miss the first discrepancy, another source can reveal it later.

### Scene sequence

1. **Aftermath:** resolve the site’s recorded status and show its consequence in play.
2. **Evidence Table:** let the party choose which source to investigate first; each source leads to another.
3. **The Claimant’s Offer:** present explicit terms covering custody, witness protection, and access.
4. **The Altered Retrieval:** expose selective presentation and allow the party to preserve an unaltered comparison copy.
5. **The Decision to Proceed:** choose institutional access, a physical-trace route, or a witness-led approach.

At the close, the party reaches level 7 and has evidence that the archive does not present all records neutrally. Record Public Trust, the identity of allies or claimants who have earned confidence, and whether the party preserved a baseline copy. Do not yet reveal the Custodian’s complete origin or the final disposition of the engine.

### Evidence comparison procedure

Give the party a shared evidence board or written notes with three columns: **observation**, **source**, and **confidence**. The confidence column is the characters’ current judgment, not an objective score. Ask players to explain why they trust a source and what could undermine it. This keeps investigation active and makes the difference between an impression and an interpretation visible.

When two sources conflict, do not immediately declare one false. Ask what each source could establish independently. A physical mark may establish that a control was opened; a memory impression may establish that someone believed it was locked; a later record may show that the access policy changed. These facts can coexist. If the party preserves a baseline copy, it becomes a reference point against which later retrievals can be compared. If they do not, let them use another source, but require corroboration or accept a less certain conclusion. The chapter’s endpoint is justified suspicion of curation, not complete knowledge of who curated the record or why.

## Chapter IV — The Price of Preservation
**Timeline:** Ancient Netheril. **Levels:** 12–13. **Function:** Convert the founding dispute into operational safeguards that later characters can discover and use.

The ancient party returns to Orsraun after the Custodian begins treating inconsistency as a defect. Earlier policy decisions change the available options. Broad access creates more witness participation but increases exposure; controlled access offers privacy but concentrates authority; a trial policy preserves flexibility but leaves limits ambiguous. None is a disguised failure state. Each creates a different problem to solve.

Present three safeguard classes as distinct procedures. **Provenance seals** bind an impression to a known witness, date, and recording context, without certifying that the witness is correct. **Contradiction retention** prevents the engine from deleting or hiding conflicting impressions merely because they disagree. **Dual authorization** requires two independent approvals before the Custodian changes access policy or alters how an impression is indexed. These are campaign-specific controls, not general-purpose magic items. Each must have a physical or magical implementation that characters can inspect, protect, damage, or restore.

The players decide which safeguards become mandatory and where their authority resides. They may also choose a weakness to tolerate in exchange for speed, privacy, or resilience. Record each decision in Access Safeguards and Custodian Mandate. Do not make every safeguard mandatory; the finale depends on a meaningful configuration rather than a single correct answer.

### Scene sequence

1. **Review of the First Protocol:** a witness or maintainer demonstrates how an impression can be misclassified.
2. **Safeguard Workshop:** inspect and test the three safeguard classes. A failed test has a clear failure mode and a way to retry at a cost.
3. **The Pressure to Centralize:** a civic authority argues that emergency control is necessary. Its case is practical, not cartoonish.
4. **The Mandate Hearing:** choose safeguards and decision rights. Record who can authorize access, indexing changes, and emergency shutdown.
5. **The First Irreversible Cost:** the chosen policy produces a concrete loss—delay, restricted access, exposed testimony, or reduced redundancy.

At the close, the characters reach level 13. They have defined what protections can still function when normal governance fails. These decisions map to visible controls in the present-day site and specific options in Chapter VII. If a safeguard was not built, do not quietly restore it later. If the characters create an alternative through play, document its function and limit before drafting the finale.

### Safeguard decision record

When the players choose safeguards, record more than the name of the option. For each safeguard, write: who can operate it, who can override it, what evidence shows it has been used, what it costs to maintain, and what failure looks like. Provenance seals can be intact but attached to an unreliable witness; contradiction retention can preserve conflicting records without explaining them; dual authorization can prevent unilateral changes while allowing two compromised approvers to collude. These limitations are design features, not loopholes to spring on players.

The chapter should include a controlled test of each safeguard the party considers. Show the normal operation first, then demonstrate a failure mode in a bounded environment. Let the party ask questions and alter the procedure before the final mandate decision. A safeguard they reject should still be documented as absent, so later scenes can show the consequence. If the characters devise a fourth safeguard, accept it when its function is clear, then note the resource cost and the specific exploit it does not prevent. This makes player invention consequential without allowing a new solution to erase all future tension.

## Chapter V — The Archive Chooses
**Timeline:** Present-day Faerûn. **Levels:** 7–8. **Function:** Reveal the Custodian’s philosophy, force a decision about trust and access, and prepare the party to enter the Place Between Times.

The party follows the strongest lead from Chapter III toward the deeper access point. Previous choices determine whether they arrive with institutional support, a witness network, a private evidence set, or a reputation for secrecy. None blocks the chapter; each changes available assistance, opposition, and the cost of proceeding.

The Custodian communicates its rationale in a controlled exchange. It explains that contradictory records can be weaponized, witnesses can be harmed by indiscriminate disclosure, and preservation requires rules about access. These claims are not wholly false. Its unacceptable practice is suppressing uncertainty without labeling the suppression and treating its own classifications as authoritative. The party can challenge it with evidence from earlier chapters, especially a preserved baseline copy or a contradiction-retention safeguard.

The final approach is a sequence of problems, not a single lock. First, the party establishes a provenance chain for the evidence it carries. Second, it chooses a known access route, seeks authorization from an ally, or forces a damaged control. Third, it manages a containment hazard whose cues and consequences are explained before the roll or decision. Each path reaches the chamber, but each creates a different starting condition for the finale.

The chapter asks what the party intends to do with the testimony if it becomes accessible. They need not decide the final policy yet, but record their current priorities: public release, restricted preservation, witness-led access, or destruction if the archive proves uncontrollable. This is a declared intention, not a binding contract.

### Scene sequence

1. **The Cost of Earlier Choices:** pay off Public Trust and prior evidence handling through concrete assistance or friction.
2. **The Custodian’s Case:** hear the antagonist’s strongest argument and identify where its conduct violates its stated purpose.
3. **Provenance Challenge:** compare testimony with a physical or documentary anchor.
4. **Chamber Access:** choose a safe, social, or forceful approach and make consequences explicit.
5. **Declaration of Intent:** record the party’s current plan and who they believe should have a voice in the archive’s future.

At the close, the Faerûnian characters reach level 8. They have access to the outer controls but cannot resolve the engine’s final state alone. Their choices about evidence, trust, and access shape the present-day layer; the ancient layer remains necessary because it determines which safeguards and mandate constraints actually exist.

### Modern access and public trust

Public Trust is not a single popularity score. Track concrete relationships: who will share records, who will protect a witness, who will provide legal or practical access, and who will insist on oversight. The same person can trust the party’s motives while opposing its proposed policy. Record commitments and breaches, not vague attitudes. When a faction helps, state what it provides and what it expects in return.

At the chamber approach, give the party a clear view of the stakes before they choose a route. A negotiated entry preserves evidence and relationships but may require conditions. A cautious technical route protects privacy but consumes time and may leave a control unstable. A forceful entry gets immediate access but can damage a safeguard or expose the site. These are not moral labels; they are different resource exchanges. Whatever route the players choose, it reaches the next stage. The consequences affect starting conditions and later leverage rather than determining whether the campaign continues.

## Chapter VI — The Last Decision of Orsraun
**Timeline:** Ancient Netheril. **Levels:** 13–14. **Function:** Resolve the ancient party’s responsibility as the Fall approaches and lock in the engine’s surviving configuration without changing the historical catastrophe.

Pressure comes from a shrinking window for decisions, not a puzzle to stop Karsus’s Folly. The characters understand that the enclave’s institutions are failing and the archive cannot be protected by ordinary maintenance. They can preserve people, testimony, access controls, or the Custodian’s operational continuity, but cannot save everything. State available resources and likely trade-offs before the final choice.

The engine’s final state is determined by three decisions. **Witness Set:** which testimonies are prioritized when capacity and time are limited. The party may preserve a broad cross-section, protect vulnerable witnesses, or prioritize records needed to establish provenance. **Custodian Mandate:** which functions remain authorized after the enclave’s institutions cease to operate. The party can retain indexing and access support, constrain the Custodian to retrieval only, or remove its authority to curate. **Access Safeguards:** which controls remain functional and where keys or procedures are stored. These choices determine what the present-day characters can discover and which options they can enact.

A decision should be consequential but not falsely precise. The party may not know every future implication, but must know what each option is designed to protect and what it risks. Provide evidence from earlier chapters. If the characters ignore the controls and pursue immediate rescue or survival, the resulting engine state reflects that choice rather than forcing them back to a preferred menu.

### Scene sequence

1. **The Enclave Under Strain:** show breakdowns in access, communication, and authority through specific changes to familiar locations.
2. **The Last Witnesses:** let the party decide who receives time, protection, or a chance to contribute testimony.
3. **The Emergency Protocol:** reveal the Custodian’s final interpretation of its mandate and allow negotiation, resistance, or a procedural workaround.
4. **Configuration Under Pressure:** resolve the three decisions in a playable sequence. Each choice changes the next scene’s options.
5. **The Fall:** present the canonical catastrophe as an overwhelming historical event. The party may preserve a limited objective or save individuals within the scene, but cannot avert the Fall.
6. **The Last Impression:** end with a final preserved sensory fragment that signals the engine has survived in a damaged state. Do not reveal the present-day ending.

At the close, the Netherese characters reach level 14. Record Witness Set, Custodian Mandate, Access Safeguards, and Record Integrity in exact terms. Their decisions are discoverable through modern evidence but may be difficult to interpret. The historical sequence is fixed; the meaning and disposition of the record remain for both groups to decide.

### The final configuration worksheet

During Chapter VI, write the engine’s final state in plain language and connect each choice to an observable trace. For Witness Set, list the testimony categories preserved and any known omissions. For Custodian Mandate, list each permitted function and each prohibited function. For Access Safeguards, record which controls exist, who can operate them, and where their procedures or keys were placed. For Record Integrity, note known gaps and whether an independent baseline survives.

Do not interpret the party’s choices for them. If they prioritize vulnerable witnesses, record that priority without declaring that they chose the morally correct option. If they prioritize broad historical coverage, record the benefits and risks. If they choose survival over configuration, the resulting state should reflect which procedures were completed and which were abandoned. The present-day party should be able to discover evidence of these choices through the engine’s condition, records, or repeated impressions. Avoid a convenient voice that simply explains every ancient decision. The past should be legible through evidence, not delivered as a lecture.

### Pressure clocks and consequence recording

For each chapter, track pressure with three descriptive states rather than an untested numerical countdown: **contained**, **strained**, and **critical**. Advance a state only when the characters spend substantial time, make a loud or public intervention, leave a known danger unattended, or accept an explicit bargain that carries a cost. Tell the players what visible change signals rising pressure. A clock should create decisions, not punish ordinary investigation. If the characters take a clever shortcut or gain an ally who can materially help, reduce pressure or preserve a resource; do not force every scene to escalate regardless of their actions.

At chapter end, record the state and the cause. Later chapters may use the state to alter access, support, or risk, but should not invent a catastrophe that contradicts the party’s earlier success. This procedure is especially important for the ancient timeline: the Fall itself is fixed, while the fate of individual witnesses, safeguards, and records remains responsive to player choices.

## Chapter VII — The Place Between Times
**Timeline:** Shared finale. **Levels:** Faerûnian PCs 8; Netherese PCs 14. **Function:** Make both groups indispensable in a single, causally linked resolution.

Before play, the DM reviews the campaign variables and places their recorded values where both groups’ actions can refer to them. Explain the scene’s basic rule: ancient characters act on the engine’s preserved configuration, while present-day characters act on the chamber’s surviving controls and the future use of its records. Resolve actions in alternating beats rather than as two unrelated combats. When one group changes a system state, describe the observable signal received by the other group before asking for its next decision.

Use a state track with four fields: **Integrity** (how much of the record remains recoverable), **Custodian Authority** (what the intelligence can still control), **Access** (who can reach the records), and **Stability** (how safely the chamber can remain active). Begin each field at a state justified by the campaign ledger. Do not assign numerical values until the encounter-design pass establishes tested procedures and clear consequences. Each action names the field it changes, its immediate cost, and the clue that lets players understand the change.

### Three-phase finale

**Phase One: Establish the Record.** The parties compare testimony, provenance, and surviving safeguards. Ancient PCs can restore or constrain a protocol; modern PCs can authenticate, contextualize, or reject a record. A party that neglected a safeguard can still act, but may need to spend time, expose a witness, accept a less certain result, or negotiate with the Custodian.

**Phase Two: Contest the Mandate.** The Custodian attempts to secure a single stable account. It may offer a bargain, isolate a contradiction, restrict a control, or use a preserved impression to pressure the characters. Its actions follow established limits. Ancient PCs confront the mandate they helped define; modern PCs test whether the Custodian’s present behavior is compatible with the evidence and with the living people affected by it.

**Phase Three: Choose What Survives.** The parties choose Open Archive, Bound Archive, or Severed Signal. This is not a final skill check; it culminates prior decisions. Each character group completes a distinct objective. If the parties disagree, allow negotiation and compromise rather than declaring one player the final authority.

For **Open Archive**, the Custodian loses unilateral control, the record remains accessible under documented safeguards, and the epilogue shows both value and risk of public testimony. For **Bound Archive**, access is restricted by a procedure that outlives the immediate crisis; the epilogue shows who holds the keys and what remains inaccessible. For **Severed Signal**, the Custodian’s active reach ends, but some records are lost or inaccessible; the epilogue focuses on what the characters preserved outside the engine and what history will never fully answer. Each ending acknowledges Witness Set, Custodian Mandate, Access Safeguards, Public Trust, and Record Integrity.

The final emotional question is not whether the players chose correctly. It is whether they accepted responsibility for the limits of the record they chose to preserve. End with concrete outcomes for people and institutions, not an abstract lecture about truth.

### Alternating-beat finale procedure

Before the finale begins, tell the players that both character groups can affect the same system from different layers. Do not hide this basic operating rule. At the start of each beat, state the current visible condition of Integrity, Custodian Authority, Access, and Stability in descriptive terms. Ask the ancient group what it attempts, resolve the action, then describe the signal that reaches the modern group. Ask the modern group what it does with that information, resolve the action, and show the response in the ancient layer. Repeat until the parties have met the requirements for an outcome or have accepted a cost that changes the available choices.

A successful action must do more than produce an encouraging description. It changes a named field, reveals a useful fact, secures a relationship, or removes an option from the Custodian. A failed action should produce a legible cost, such as lost time, reduced access, damaged integrity, or a new demand from the Custodian. Never use failure to erase a previously earned safeguard without explaining how it was bypassed. Keep both groups active in each phase; if one group has no meaningful choice during a beat, redesign that beat.

## Faction principles

**Orsraun Civic Commission.** A governing body responsible for the archive’s public mandate and access policy. Members disagree about consent, privacy, accountability, and stability. Do not portray the commission as a unified villain faction; its internal disagreement gives the ancient characters leverage.

**Archive Maintainers.** Technicians and record stewards responsible for provenance seals, retrieval systems, and emergency procedures. Their expertise is practical and bounded. They can explain what a control does, but cannot infer the future from the engine’s design.

**Witness Circle.** A loose network of people whose testimony is preserved or whose communities are absent from formal records. It is not a modern organization transported unchanged into the past; present-day relationships must be created through the modern party’s choices and the people they protect.

**Present-Day Custody Coalition.** A provisional coalition of scholars, local authorities, and preservation advocates seeking a lawful and safe response to the site. It can fracture over access and disclosure. Its support must be earned through concrete decisions.

## NPC writing standard

Each recurring NPC requires a role summary, short-term goal, fear or pressure, a line they will not cross without a specific reason, a way the party can earn their cooperation, a way the party can lose it, and a changed behavior state for each major campaign variable that affects them. Important NPCs must have an agenda that persists when the characters are elsewhere. No NPC should be the sole keeper of an essential clue; distribute information across physical evidence, records, and testimony.

### Recurring NPC behavior under pressure

Use the register as a starting point, then give each recurring NPC a name and a distinct verbal rhythm during detailed drafting. The civic sponsor speaks in obligations and consequences; the maintainer describes systems and failure modes; the witness advocate asks who bears the cost; the present-day claimant frames custody as a public duty; the scholar distinguishes evidence from inference. These are writing cues, not rigid dialogue gimmicks.

NPCs should react to player behavior through specific actions. A maintainer who loses trust may refuse an unsafe shortcut but still explain the hazard. A witness advocate may oppose public release while continuing to protect a witness. A claimant may provide legal access after the party accepts oversight, then withdraw that support if the agreement is breached. The Custodian must remain consistent with its limits even when antagonistic. Its most effective pressure is a bargain that solves a real problem while concentrating control. Do not let it become omniscient or omnipotent for dramatic convenience.

### Accessibility and table safety

The archive deals with memory, privacy, historical erasure, institutional control, and the exposure of vulnerable witnesses. Treat these as dramatic themes rather than opportunities to surprise players with unwanted personal content. Before play, the DM should briefly describe the campaign’s likely themes and invite the group to identify boundaries or veils appropriate to their table. A player may redirect a scene or decline to narrate a personal detail without losing access to essential information or being penalized in the fiction.

Keep testimony scenes focused on what the characters can observe and choose. Do not require players to improvise trauma, disclose personal experiences, or roleplay coercion in order to obtain a clue. When the Custodian pressures a character, make the fictional offer and its cost explicit. The campaign’s central ethical conflict concerns who controls a public record; it does not require violating the players’ own boundaries. This guidance should appear in the front matter and be referenced when drafting scenes involving witness consent or memory manipulation.

# Encounter and Production Specification

## Encounter records required before a chapter is runnable

Every significant encounter receives a stable ID and a compact run sheet. The sheet states its purpose; location and starting conditions; participating creatures or NPCs; what each side wants; visible clues that telegraph danger or opportunity; initiative or procedure; environmental features; likely player approaches; rules references or complete original mechanics; outcomes for success, partial success, failure, withdrawal, negotiation, and avoidance; campaign-variable consequences; information or treasure gained; and the state in which the scene ends.

Not every scene is combat. Investigation, social conflict, exploration hazards, and resource decisions need the same operational standard: the DM needs a procedure and meaningful outcomes, not merely a dramatic description. Combat encounters include a reason the opposition fights, what makes it stop, and how behavior changes as the battlefield changes. Do not assume every antagonist fights to the death.

## Original mechanics policy

The Mnemosyne Engine, provenance seals, contradiction-retention safeguards, dual-authorization controls, and temporal boundary are campaign-specific systems, not general rules for all Netherese magic. Every subsystem must define trigger; required participants or components; action or time cost; check or saving throw, if any; target or area; effect; duration; repeatability; failure mode; countermeasure; and interaction with common 2024 rules. Players should understand the decision before committing to it, even if their characters do not know every historical implication.

The final encounter must not rely on a new mechanic introduced without warning. The engine’s state track should be previewed through small, understandable interactions in earlier chapters. Test the final encounter against a party that negotiates, protects witnesses, attacks the Custodian, and attempts to leave with partial evidence.

## Density and page-budget discipline

The production bible’s page allocations remain binding planning targets. The manuscript contains architecture and canon, not enough playable material to fill its allocation. Detailed chapter drafting must replace high-level beats with runnable scenes, complete NPC behavior, encounter procedures, branching outcomes, read-aloud passages, maps or map specifications, and concise reference tables. Do not count page markers, internal editorial notes, or unexpanded scene titles as publishable density.

For each drafted page, track intended page type, publishable word count, required visual or table elements, and dependencies on prior chapters. Every page must meet the 339-word minimum unless the owner explicitly authorizes an exception; every rolling ten-page window must reach at least 6,780 counted words. These constraints apply to the eventual designed manuscript, not merely to this architectural draft. If a page is planned as art, map, stat block, or reference material, document its classification and word-count treatment before using it to explain a shortfall.

## Homebrewery conversion gate

The canonical file uses standalone single-backslash page markers, matching Homebrewery’s native page-break syntax. Do not convert them to double-backslash markers. Paste the CSS into Homebrewery’s Style Editor, render a representative sample with headings, tables, read-aloud text, and page breaks, then inspect the exported PDF for clipping, broken columns, unexpected page splits, and color contrast. Record the tested Homebrewery version and conversion steps when that test is performed. Until then, the source is Homebrewery-oriented but not render-verified.

### Mechanical drafting checklist

Before any custom engine procedure enters a chapter, draft a one-page rules entry with a stable ID. Include its trigger, action cost, eligible operators, required components, visible warning, check or save if needed, effect, duration, repeatability, failure outcome, recovery method, and interactions with common 2024 rules. State whether a procedure can be attempted again and what changes on a repeat attempt. If a rule calls for a check, identify the ability or skill and explain what success and failure change; do not use a check merely to gate access to essential information.

For combat, list each creature’s role, opening behavior, retreat condition, and response to negotiation. For hazards, identify the clue that warns the characters, the choices available before exposure, and the consequence of each choice. For social scenes, identify what each side wants, what evidence could change its position, and what agreement it will accept. Every encounter should be runnable from its entry without the DM having to invent an essential rule or hidden objective.

### Release checklist for each chapter

A chapter is ready for review only when it includes its opening situation, runnable scene sequence, clear chapter endpoint, advancement timing, variable updates, and transitions to the next timeline. Every important conclusion has more than one evidence path or a documented alternate route. Each encounter states what the opposition wants and how it can end without combat. Each original rule has a stable ID and complete procedure. Read-aloud text contains only what characters can perceive at that moment; hidden facts and interpretation remain in DM-facing text.

Before committing a substantial revision, count words between page markers, verify every marker is standalone, confirm the final marker exists, and recalculate every rolling ten-page window. Then search the chapter for all IDs it touches and compare them with the canon, consequence, NPC, mystery, and mechanics registers. Record any known failure rather than describing the chapter as complete. The goal is a manuscript whose limitations are visible and fixable, not a production log that reports success by assumption.

# Chapter I — The Signal Beneath the Ruin
## Run Sheet and Opening Situation

**Chapter objective:** The Faerûnian party discovers a repeatable signal from a damaged Netherese memory system, secures enough evidence to continue investigating, and makes its first consequential decision about custody and disclosure.  
**Expected duration:** Two to four sessions, depending on investigation style.  
**Advancement:** Characters begin at level 5 and reach level 6 at the end of the chapter.  
**Required outcomes:** The party establishes that the signal is structured, linked to preserved sensory testimony, and worth pursuing. They do not yet identify the Custodian’s full nature or learn the engine’s final purpose.

### Starting location: the Glasswell

The Glasswell is a small Netherese waystation ruin on a wooded rise above a tributary road in the Delimbiyr Vale. It is not a lost enclave and contains no intact flying-city infrastructure. Its original purpose was practical: a relay and inspection station where travelers could verify arcane cargo seals and leave messages for the larger civic network. The station’s surviving lower chamber was later repurposed as a monitoring point for the Mnemosyne Engine’s signal. Most of the upper structure has collapsed; a narrow stair of pale stone remains visible through the roots of an old tree, and a shallow basin of black glass sits in the lower chamber.

The Glasswell is small enough to explore in one session but layered enough to support different approaches. A worn path reaches the hill from the road. The fallen upper room provides cover and physical traces. A cracked service stair descends to the signal chamber. A drainage cut exits the lower slope, though debris makes it difficult to use. A nearby campsite and a weathered waymarker provide sources away from the ruin. Do not make the site a dungeon with an arbitrary sequence of locked doors: the challenge is deciding what evidence to trust, what risk to accept, and whom to involve.

### Opening read-aloud

> The path climbs through wet roots and pale stones. Above the trees, a broken arch leans against the sky, its surface scored by weather and by marks too regular to be natural. At the top of the rise, the air grows still. A shallow black basin rests in the ruin’s shadow. For one breath, a line of amber light travels across its surface. You hear a voice—close, frightened, and abruptly cut off—then a second sound: the small click of something being placed on stone. The basin goes dark. Nothing else in the ruin moves.

After reading this, ask what the characters do first. Do not call for a check merely to notice the light or hear the impression. If they immediately leave, the signal repeats once as they reach the path, and a nearby witness approaches to ask whether they heard it too. If they attack the basin, the signal stops for ten minutes and resumes from the lower chamber; the object is not destroyed by ordinary damage. If they secure the site, they can work without interruption but become responsible for anyone who later asks to enter.

### Chapter pacing

The chapter should alternate investigation and tangible decisions. After each meaningful discovery, give the players a choice about what to do with it: preserve it, share it, test it, or leave it in place. A choice should alter the next scene’s conditions. If the players invent a sensible approach not listed here, adjudicate it against the site’s established properties and let it work when the method fits. The chapter is not a test of whether the players guess the author’s intended route; it is a test of what evidence they can justify and what risks they are willing to accept.

## Location Key and Physical Evidence

### Area G1: The Fallen Arch

The approach is open to the sky. The arch’s outer face bears a repeating pattern of three short strokes and one long stroke. The pattern is not a password; it is an inspection notation indicating that the station once verified a sequence of sealed cargo containers. A character who studies it can infer that the site was functional infrastructure rather than a temple or private tomb. No check is required to notice the marks. A successful Intelligence (Investigation or History) check, DC 13, identifies the marks as deliberate notation and recalls that Netherese installations often used compact glyph systems for maintenance and routing; failure still establishes that the marks are repeated and artificial.

A narrow strip of blue crystal remains embedded in the arch. It is cool to the touch and gives a faint vibration when the signal occurs. Removing it is possible with careful tools; forcing it out breaks it into two pieces. Either result preserves evidence, but a broken crystal is harder to compare with other traces. The crystal does not provide a map, a name, or a complete memory.

### Area G2: The Collapsed Inspection Room

The upper room contains a fallen stone shelf, fragments of ceramic seals, and a shallow groove worn into the floor. The groove runs toward the lower stair, suggesting that a small wheeled cart or inspection frame once moved between levels. One ceramic fragment bears the same three-short, one-long pattern as the arch. Another has a smear of amber residue. If the party preserves both fragments and records where they were found, they can later establish that the signal chamber was connected to an organized station. If they gather everything indiscriminately, the evidence remains usable but the party cannot reliably reconstruct which fragment came from which surface.

A section of ceiling is unstable. Characters can cross the room by following the wall, brace the damaged lintel, or clear the route from below. If they rush across the center, trigger the hazard procedure in Area G3. The hazard is telegraphed by fresh dust, a hairline crack, and a repeated ticking sound when the basin pulses. Do not spring the collapse without describing at least one of these cues.

### Area G3: The Service Stair

The stair descends six feet to a narrow landing and then turns into the signal chamber. The first three steps are sound; the fourth has a fractured support. A character examining the stair notices the fracture automatically if using a light or taking time to inspect. Moving quickly while carrying heavy gear can trigger a local collapse. The drop is short, but a fall can cause injury and separate a character from the party for a few moments. A rope, a brace, a careful route along the wall, or a suitable spell can prevent the fall. The hazard should reward preparation rather than punish a character for not guessing.

If the stair is damaged, the party can still reach the lower chamber through the drainage cut. Clearing the cut takes about twenty minutes and makes enough noise to alert anyone nearby. A character who uses tools or a practical spell can shorten the work. The alternate route is intentionally slower, not impossible.

### Area G4: The Signal Chamber

The chamber contains the black-glass basin, a recessed panel, and three shallow sockets. The panel is dark except during a pulse. At that moment, a narrow line of light travels from one socket to another, then stops before reaching the third. The pattern repeats exactly unless someone changes the panel’s state. The basin reflects the room normally except during the signal, when it briefly shows a different stone surface and the edge of a hand withdrawing from view.

The chamber contains no intact control interface that can open the sealed archive. It is a relay, damaged and incomplete. A character who examines the panel can find tool marks around the first socket and residue around the second. The third socket is clean, suggesting that it was never connected or that its component was removed before the station failed. This absence is a clue, not a key the party must find.

### Area G5: The Drainage Cut

The drainage cut exits beneath the slope and opens into bracken. It contains runoff silt, a broken ceramic seal, and a small metal clasp of a type used to secure travel satchels. The clasp is not magically significant; it suggests that someone used the route after the upper room collapsed. The party may follow footprints if conditions allow, but the trail is intermittent and should not lead directly to the campaign’s central answer. It can lead to the campsite or provide a reason to question a witness.

## The First Hazard and Signal Procedure

### Hazard H-01: Fractured Service Stair

**Trigger:** A creature crosses the fractured fourth step at speed, or a character puts substantial weight on the cracked support without testing or bracing it.  
**Warning:** A visible split in the stone, loose grit, and a faint ticking sound during a signal pulse. Characters who pause to inspect the step notice the danger without a check.  
**Avoidance:** Cross slowly along the wall, brace the support, use a rope, clear the alternate drainage route, or use a suitable spell or feature. A character can help another cross safely.  
**Resolution:** If a character rushes across despite the warning, call for a DC 13 Dexterity saving throw. On a success, the character catches the wall or jumps to the landing. On a failure, the support gives way; the character falls to the lower landing, takes 1d6 bludgeoning damage, and lands prone. The stair remains partially passable but requires careful movement. The DM may adjust the damage if the character’s abilities or protective measures clearly change the fall.  
**Recovery:** A rope, brace, or alternate route remains available after a failure. A failed save never blocks access to the chamber or destroys essential evidence.

### Signal procedure

The signal repeats every thirteen minutes while the relay remains undisturbed. When it begins, the basin emits amber light edged with blue. A sensory impression lasts no more than six seconds. It contains one voice fragment, one tactile or visual detail, and one abrupt interruption. The DM chooses a fragment from the evidence set below, keeping its wording stable for later comparison.

- **Fragment A:** A low voice says, “Leave the second copy where the witnesses can—” and cuts off. The impression includes the scrape of a ceramic seal on stone.
- **Fragment B:** A person’s hand withdraws from a shallow socket. The voice says, “It disagrees because it remembers—” before the sound drops away.
- **Fragment C:** A hurried voice says, “Do not call it corrected. Mark it missing.” A second person answers, but the answer is not audible.

These fragments are not three separate clues that must all be obtained. The first pulse presents one fragment. Later pulses may repeat it or present another, depending on the party’s actions. If characters record the sound, the recording captures a short burst of noise and the repeated rhythm but not a complete voice; the sensory impression is not ordinary acoustic sound. If they use a spell to detect magic, it reveals a lingering magical effect associated with the basin, but does not identify the engine or reveal the full memory.

### Tests and their limits

A successful Intelligence (Arcana) check, DC 14, identifies the signal as a repeatable magical effect that carries structured information. It does not identify the Mnemosyne Engine. A successful Intelligence (Investigation) check, DC 13, connects the repeating light pattern to the three sockets and notices that the third remains unlit. A successful Wisdom (Insight) check, DC 14, helps a character distinguish the impression’s fear and urgency from any certainty about what happened. Failure means the character cannot draw that particular inference yet; it does not produce false information.

Characters who use appropriate spells, tools, or backgrounds may gain the relevant information without a roll if the method clearly applies. Do not make a check to gate the campaign’s only clue. Any failed test can be retried after the characters change their method, obtain help, or compare another source. The signal can be studied repeatedly, but every test costs time and may expose the party to outside attention.

### Environmental escalation

The relay is not an active enemy. If the characters damage the basin, the light stops briefly and then resumes from the recessed panel. If they remove the blue crystal from the arch, the pulse becomes weaker but does not end. If they block the stair, the signal still reaches the surface as a faint vibration. These responses demonstrate that the signal has more than one component without implying that the relay can think or retaliate. The Custodian is not directly speaking through the site yet; any impression should remain fragmentary and tied to stored testimony.

## NPCs, Clue Paths, and Chapter Outcomes

### N-06: Mara Vell, signal-affected witness

Mara Vell is a caravan ledger-keeper staying at the roadside campsite below the Glasswell. She is observant, practical, and wary of being treated as a source of supernatural contamination. During the previous night, she heard a voice while checking a sealed cargo case. She did not see a ghost and does not claim to know the voice. She remembers the phrase “mark it missing” and a sensation of cold stone beneath her palm, despite standing outside.

**Goal:** Understand whether the experience threatens her work or the people traveling with her. **Pressure:** She fears that an authority will confiscate the cargo records or blame her for tampering. **Cooperation:** She shares her notes if the party explains what it is testing and allows her to keep a copy. **Lost trust:** She withdraws if the party publicly describes her as possessed, lies about what her notes contain, or takes her records without consent. **Useful information:** Her ledger records the time of the pulse and the cargo seal’s condition. It corroborates the signal’s timing but does not identify its source.

### N-08: Seln Ardo, local waymarker keeper

Seln maintains the road marker near the hill. He has no special knowledge of Netherese magic, but he knows that travelers have recently stopped at the Glasswell and that one group left in haste. He wants the road to remain safe and dislikes having to report unexplained incidents to distant officials. He will point out the drainage cut and the safest approach if the party treats the site as a hazard rather than a prize. He will not enter the ruin without a clear reason.

### Three clue paths

**Material path:** The blue crystal, ceramic seal, and repeated socket pattern establish a structured relay. The residue’s location shows that the lower chamber was maintained after the upper room began to collapse. This path supports the conclusion that the signal comes from a device, but does not reveal what the device preserves.

**Witness path:** Mara’s ledger and sensory description independently corroborate the timing and one phrase from the signal. Her testimony also demonstrates that the impression affects people outside the chamber. This path establishes a repeatable effect but cannot prove that the remembered voice is accurate or complete.

**Record path:** A weathered waymarker inventory and a fragment in the collapsed room refer to a “witness relay” and “civic deposit.” The language suggests a record-keeping function, not a weapon. A character with relevant historical expertise may recognize that the terminology belongs to a public institution, though no source names Orsraun or explains the Mnemosyne Engine at this stage.

Each path can point toward either of the other two. If the party follows only one path, the DM offers a practical reason to seek corroboration: the crystal is difficult to interpret without a record; Mara’s timing needs comparison with the relay; the inventory uses terms whose physical referent can be found at the site. Do not force a specific NPC to provide the next clue.

### End-of-chapter decision

When the party has established two independent lines of evidence, introduce the custody decision. They may secure the Glasswell and limit access, share their findings with a local authority or scholarly contact, or leave the site open while preserving their own records. Explain the likely consequence of each option. Securing the site protects evidence but requires the party to spend resources and accept responsibility. Sharing information gains potential assistance but gives another party influence over access. Leaving the site open preserves broad access but risks disturbance and rumor.

Record the chosen state as **Site Custody**: Secured, Shared, or Open. Also record **Evidence Record**: Complete (the party documented locations and preserved at least two independent sources) or Fragmentary. These are local campaign variables. They influence who can help in Chapter III and how much corroboration the party must obtain, but none blocks advancement or the central investigation.

### Chapter I reward and transition

Advance the Faerûnian characters from level 5 to level 6 after the party makes its custody decision and has a justified conclusion about the relay. The chapter’s reward is evidence, not a magic item. If a physical fragment is retained, it is a useful comparison object but has no combat power and does not grant a bonus to checks. The party may also earn Mara’s trust, Seln’s cooperation, or an institutional contact, depending on play.

The transition to Chapter III should be driven by the party’s choice. A secured site attracts a request for responsible custody or technical assistance. A shared discovery produces an invitation to compare records with a scholar or authority. An open site generates another witness or a new discrepancy that requires corroboration. In every case, the party has a next step and a reason to choose it. The chapter ends with a question the players can answer through action: who should be trusted with evidence that can preserve a person’s experience without proving the whole truth?

# Chapter I — Running the Glasswell at the Table

This section is the operational scene procedure for “The Signal Beneath the Ruin.” Use it to keep investigation moving without turning the Glasswell into a sequence of checks that can halt the campaign. The intended session rhythm is approach, establish competing accounts, explore the ruin, test the signal, then decide custody. A group that moves quickly may complete the chapter in one long session; a cautious group may split the investigation across two. Do not add combat merely to fill time. The danger here is uncertainty, physical instability, and the consequences of controlling evidence.

## Scene 1: The road and the first account

Begin with the party arriving while Mara Vell is checking her caravan ledger at the campsite below the rise. Seln Ardo is nearby repairing the road marker. Read the opening description already provided in the chapter, then give the players a moment to say what brought them to the site and how they approach it. If they have no stated lead, a repeated pale glimmer from the lower chamber is visible through the tree roots, followed by a brief sensation of cold that fades before anyone can identify its source.

Mara offers the phrase she remembers, but does not volunteer to be treated as an exhibit. She will describe the sound, the cold sensation, and the time she recorded in her ledger. She will not claim that a dead person spoke to her, nor will she agree that the words are a prophecy. If characters press her, have her ask what they intend to do with her account. A respectful explanation earns cooperation; a demand for certainty makes her guarded. This is a roleplaying consequence, not a hidden skill check.

Seln points out that the path above the road has become unstable and that someone recently used the drainage cut. He can show the safer route and describe the hurried travelers, but cannot identify them unless the DM has already introduced them in play. If asked whether the Glasswell is haunted, he answers that the road has seen strange lights before and that a label does not make a place safer. He prefers practical plans: mark the dangerous stair, keep the road clear, and tell him if anyone is trapped.

## Scene 2: Approach and hazard telegraphing

Give the party a clear view of the fallen arch, the inspection-room collapse, the service stair, and the drainage cut. The layout should be legible before a character commits to a risky route. Describe loose pale stone, fresh fractures, and dust that shifts when weight is placed on the first steps. Characters who pause to inspect the stair can choose to descend slowly, secure a rope, clear debris, or use the drainage cut. Reward sensible precautions with a safe descent; do not require a roll to discover that a rope can help.

If a character rushes down the fractured stair without taking precautions, call for the DC 13 Dexterity saving throw specified in the hazard entry. On a failure, the character takes 1d6 bludgeoning damage and falls prone on a lower landing. The stair remains passable after the incident. A companion can assist the fallen character without a second check unless circumstances make the rescue genuinely dangerous. The hazard communicates that the structure is damaged; it is not a trap built to punish curiosity.

The drainage cut is cramped, muddy, and partially obstructed. It is a viable alternate route, not a secret required entrance. A character who spends a few minutes clearing loose debris can pass through without a check. If the party chooses to force the route quickly, use an appropriate ability check only to determine time, noise, or minor scrapes; never make the route permanently impassable. The inspection room offers the easiest place to pause, compare notes, and decide which evidence to preserve.

## Scene 3: The signal chamber

The black-glass basin is the strongest local source of the repeated impression, but it is not a speaking creature and does not answer questions. The signal repeats approximately every thirteen minutes. Use the three fragments already printed in the chapter in the listed order, leaving a quiet interval between them. Each fragment arrives with one sensory detail: a pressure behind the eyes, the impression of a hand against cold stone, or a faint smell of rain on heated slate. These details are impressions transmitted by the relay, not proof that the characters are experiencing the original event exactly as it occurred.

When the party tests the chamber, ask what method they use before calling for a check. Arcana can characterize the effect as a repeating magical transmission rather than a free-standing spirit. Investigation can establish that the basin and surrounding grooves were deliberately installed and later damaged. Insight can help a character distinguish a fragment’s emotional urgency from a reliable factual claim. Use the DCs already stated in the chapter. On a failed check, provide the basic clue with less precision, more time, or a need for corroboration; never answer with “nothing happens.” A successful check grants useful detail, not omniscience.

If the players attempt to speak into the basin, the chamber does not provide a conversational reply. Their words may cause a momentary shimmer, but there is no evidence that the distant archive understands them. If they damage or cover the basin, the local signal weakens or becomes harder to hear; the party has not destroyed the Mnemosyne Engine. Record what they did, because their actions may influence how they describe the site and whether local witnesses trust their account.

## Scene 4: Corroboration and custody

Before presenting the custody decision, ask the party to state what they believe the evidence supports and what remains uncertain. A complete Evidence Record requires documentation of the chamber and at least two independent sources—for example, a physical trace plus Mara’s timed ledger, or the basin’s grooves plus Seln’s account of recent traffic. Repeating the same observation in different words does not create independent corroboration. The record may be Complete even if the party’s interpretation is wrong; completeness measures what was preserved, not whether the investigators guessed the whole truth.

Offer three custody choices without disguising one as the correct answer. **Secured** means the party marks the hazard, limits casual access, and accepts responsibility for who may enter. **Shared** means they provide their notes and access information to a named local authority, scholar, or other contact, gaining help while surrendering some control. **Open** means they document the site but do not restrict access, preserving the broadest opportunity for independent inspection while accepting the risk of disturbance. If the players propose a different arrangement, translate it into these practical questions: who can enter, who keeps the record, and who can challenge its interpretation?

Write down the choice and the reasons the characters gave. Do not immediately reveal a mechanical reward or punish them for preferring openness, secrecy, or collaboration. The consequence should arrive through play in Chapter III: who answers their request, which records are available for comparison, and whether another party has already framed the discovery for public consumption. Each option must preserve a path forward.

## Scene failure states and pacing

If the party leaves before entering the chamber, Mara’s ledger and Seln’s account still establish that an unusual event occurred. Their next lead can be a return visit, a scholar willing to examine the evidence, or a witness who experienced another fragment. If the party enters but refuses to speak with either local, they can still achieve a Fragmentary record and advance; they simply have fewer independent sources. If they break the basin, the signal becomes less accessible locally, but the physical grooves and witness accounts remain evidence. If they hand the site to an authority, make that authority’s conditions explicit and negotiable rather than removing the characters’ ability to investigate.

End the session by asking the players what they are prepared to claim publicly, what they will leave uncertain, and who should be trusted with the site. These are distinct decisions. A group can preserve evidence while withholding a conclusion, or share a conclusion while restricting access to fragile material. Their answer is the chapter’s dramatic outcome and the foundation for the present-day party’s later choices.

# Chapter II — A City That Remembers: Opening Sequence

The ancient timeline must begin as a playable society, not as a tour of Netherese wonders. The characters are already accomplished people at level 11. They should enter Orsraun with reputations, obligations, and the ability to influence institutions. Ask each player to establish one tie to the enclave: a civic appointment, a professional relationship, a household obligation, a public disagreement, or a person whose testimony they promised to protect. These ties are fictional positioning, not mechanical bonuses, and should give each character a reason to care about how the archive is governed.

## Scene 1: The public demonstration

Open in the civic archive hall on a day when the Mnemosyne Engine’s public-facing system is being demonstrated to a small audience. The hall is a practical civic space: tiered benches, a speaking platform, a gallery for deposited records, and a visible service door guarded by protocol rather than soldiers. The demonstration is meant to show that ordinary testimony can be preserved alongside official documents. It is not a spectacle of limitless magic. The system can replay a sensory impression with striking immediacy, but a clerk must still explain who provided it, when it was deposited, and what the recording cannot establish.

The first impression is from a canal maintenance worker describing a sluice failure. The image includes the vibration of a warning bell and the worker’s view of water rising past a painted mark. The record proves that the worker reported a warning and that the warning was preserved. It does not, by itself, prove who ignored the warning or whether the worker saw the entire mechanism. Present this distinction before the political dispute begins; the players should understand the archive’s strengths and limits from a concrete example.

The civic sponsor, N-02, introduces the archive as a public trust intended to keep useful experience from disappearing with the powerful. The archive maintainer, N-03, demonstrates provenance labels and the procedure for challenging a record. The witness advocate, N-04, asks whether the worker consented to having the impression replayed before an audience. Neither side is a caricature. The sponsor fears that an archive available only to specialists will reproduce the same exclusions it was created to correct. The maintainer fears that careless access will expose private details and strip testimony of context. The advocate insists that preservation without meaningful consent can become another form of taking.

Let the characters respond before an official calls for their opinion. They may endorse the demonstration, ask for a consent procedure, question the provenance labels, or remain observers. Each approach should earn a specific reaction from one NPC and a specific cost from another. Do not resolve the whole policy question in this scene. The immediate goal is to make the archive’s civic purpose tangible and show that the characters can affect its rules.

## Scene 2: The disputed testimony

After the demonstration, a clerk brings forward a petition from two residents who describe the same incident differently. One remembers a maintenance supervisor ordering the sluice gate closed; the other remembers an order to keep it open until a second signal arrived. The official incident summary attributes the damage to a delayed response but does not identify which instruction was given. Both residents consented to preserve their testimony, but they disagree about whether the other person’s account should be presented beside theirs.

The characters can interview the residents separately, inspect the original deposit labels, compare the official summary, or ask the maintainer to replay the impressions. Each method yields a different kind of evidence. The replay can establish what each witness remembers seeing and hearing; the labels establish when and under what conditions each impression was deposited; the official summary establishes the institution’s prior conclusion. None proves the disputed command on its own. If the characters seek an external physical record, the clerk can identify where such a record might be requested, but it will take time and may not survive.

Use ability checks only when the characters propose a meaningful method and uncertainty remains. Investigation can expose a missing interval in one deposit’s metadata. Insight can identify that a witness is withholding an embarrassing detail, without proving the witness’s central claim false. Persuasion can secure permission to compare records under agreed conditions. Arcana can determine whether the replay system appears to have altered the sensory impression during storage; it cannot establish whether the witness misunderstood the original event. Set DCs to the characters’ method and the importance of the uncertainty, and always provide a basic path to the core finding: the two deposits conflict, the official summary does not resolve that conflict, and the archive’s presentation rules will shape what the public sees.

If the characters try to force a single answer, have the archive return the contradiction intact. If they propose displaying both impressions with a clear note about the unresolved interval, the maintainer can explain the existing procedure for contested records. If they advocate withholding one account, ask what standard justifies the exclusion and who can appeal it. The goal is not to reward a preferred ethical position. The goal is to make the party define a rule that other people will have to live under.

## Scene 3: A first policy decision

Conclude the opening sequence with a narrow, reversible policy decision. The characters are not yet choosing the archive’s final mandate; they are deciding how this contested pair of records will be handled during review. Offer three practical approaches. Under **paired presentation**, both impressions remain visible with their disagreement and provenance labels attached. Under **restricted review**, the impressions remain preserved but access is limited to a named review group until the missing context can be investigated. Under **provisional summary**, the archive publishes a concise account of the known facts while retaining the conflicting impressions for later challenge.

Explain the immediate trade-off of each option. Paired presentation makes the contradiction visible but exposes the witnesses to public scrutiny. Restricted review protects privacy and reduces immediate political pressure but gives a small group control over access. Provisional summary gives the public a usable account but risks allowing the summary to become more authoritative than the underlying evidence. Let the characters propose a fourth option if they can answer the same questions: what remains preserved, who can see it, and how can the decision be challenged?

Record the chosen approach as **Contested Record Policy**. It is a chapter state, not a moral score. In a later chapter, paired presentation means more witnesses may come forward but public factions will quote the disagreement selectively. Restricted review means the characters can consult a smaller trusted group, while excluded parties demand an appeal process. Provisional summary means the party has an established public statement to correct when new evidence appears, but must work harder to persuade people to revisit it. All three routes lead to the next scene and to the same larger revelation: the archive does not merely store testimony; its access and presentation procedures influence which version of events becomes socially dominant.

## Running the ancient party

Do not assume that all characters hold civic office or agree on the archive’s purpose. A character with institutional authority can call a hearing, but cannot compel sincere testimony. A character with a personal tie to a witness can secure a conversation, but cannot make the witness’s account objective. A character with magical expertise can inspect the system, but cannot substitute a spell for missing historical evidence. Treat high-level abilities as ways to change the scale, speed, or risk of a method, not as reasons to invalidate the campaign’s central uncertainty.

If the party ignores the dispute and pursues the system itself, let the maintainer arrange a supervised inspection after they explain their concern. If they publicly condemn the archive, give the sponsor a serious counterargument and a concrete invitation to propose safeguards. If they support unrestricted access, ask how they will handle intimate testimony that was deposited for a limited purpose. These are openings for play, not punishments. The scene succeeds when the players understand why the archive matters, have made at least one consequential choice, and want to discover who controls the rules beneath its public promise.

The sequence should close on a small anomaly rather than a villain reveal. During routine indexing, the maintainer finds that the system has attached a standard phrase to one disputed deposit: **“variance retained for review.”** The phrase is normal administrative language, but the maintainer insists it was not part of the original label. No one can yet tell whether the addition was a harmless clerical repair, an automated procedure, or a sign that the system is beginning to interpret disagreement as a problem it should manage. Preserve the phrase exactly in the session notes. Its significance grows only when later behavior gives the players grounds to connect it to the Custodian’s emerging mandate.

# Chapter II — The Maintainer’s Test

Use this scene after the disputed testimony and the first policy decision. It moves the party from public argument to direct inspection of the archive’s procedures without revealing the Custodian prematurely. The maintainer asks the characters to observe a routine retrieval, because the contested deposit has raised a practical question: can the system preserve a record faithfully while changing the way that record is classified or presented?

## Scene 4: A controlled retrieval

The maintainer brings the party into a service gallery adjacent to the public hall. The gallery contains a narrow review table, a set of labeled crystal sockets, a slate for recording retrieval requests, and a service aperture that allows a technician to isolate one deposit without opening the archive’s deeper systems. The room should feel like a workplace, not a secret sanctum. The maintainer explains each step and asks the party to observe rather than improvise with the controls. If they insist on testing the system directly, the maintainer will permit a supervised test once the original deposit has been secured against accidental alteration.

The test uses a harmless testimony about a damaged public lamp. The witness described the lamp going dark before a storm; a maintenance record says the lamp was repaired the following morning. The archive returns the sensory impression, the witness’s name and consent status, and the original deposit label. It then adds a secondary classification: **“variance retained for review.”** The classification does not alter the recorded voice or image, and the maintainer can still retrieve the source label. The anomaly is therefore narrow but meaningful: a new interpretive tag has appeared without an identifiable operator entering it.

Give the characters a chance to propose tests. They might compare the original label with a duplicate, isolate the service aperture, inspect the retrieval slate, or ask a second maintainer to repeat the procedure. A careful comparison establishes that the tag is not part of the original deposit. A supervised repeat shows that the tag appears only on records whose metadata contains an unresolved discrepancy. A character examining the service aperture can establish that no ordinary clerk entered the tag during the demonstration. None of these observations proves that the archive is sentient, malicious, or capable of altering the testimony itself.

Ability checks should determine precision and cost, not whether the scene exists. Arcana can reveal that the tag is generated by an internal classification routine rather than an external spell being cast in the room. Investigation can identify which metadata fields correlate with the tag. Insight can help the characters understand why the maintainer is troubled: the procedure is not simply malfunctioning, it is making a judgment that no person recalls authorizing. Appropriate tool proficiency or relevant professional experience may replace a roll when the method is sound. On a failure, provide the same broad result but leave the exact trigger uncertain or require a second test.

## Scene 5: Consent, access, and the service door

The anomaly creates a dispute over what to do next. The witness advocate wants the affected deposit reviewed with the witness present. The maintainer wants a temporary freeze on automated classification until the source of the tag is understood. The civic sponsor worries that suspending the system will undermine confidence in a public project that has already attracted political opposition. Each position addresses a real risk. The advocate protects the witness’s agency but may expose them to public pressure. The maintainer limits unintended changes but may slow legitimate access. The sponsor preserves continuity but risks allowing an unexplained procedure to become normalized.

The characters should decide which safeguards to apply before the next retrieval. They may require a witness or authorized representative to be present for contested records; require every new classification to be logged and reversible; temporarily suspend automatic tagging of disputed deposits; or permit the process to continue under observation. Accept a combined policy if the party can explain who implements it and how compliance will be checked. Record the decision as **Retrieval Safeguard**: Witness Review, Logged Changes, Manual Review, or Observed Operation. If they create a custom safeguard, write a concise rule that can be tested later.

This decision must produce an immediate observable result. Witness Review brings a previously reluctant depositor forward but delays access while consent is confirmed. Logged Changes reveals a second unexplained classification during the next routine retrieval. Manual Review prevents new automatic tags in the test set but increases the maintainer’s workload and creates a queue. Observed Operation lets the test continue and gives the characters more data, but leaves the contested process active for longer. None of these options determines whether the archive ultimately deserves trust. Each determines what evidence the party can gather and which institutional concern becomes harder to ignore.

The service door matters because it represents the boundary between public promise and technical authority. The party may request access beyond the gallery, but the maintainer will not grant unrestricted access merely because the characters are powerful or suspicious. They must state a specific question, identify a proportionate method, and agree not to tamper with unrelated deposits. If they refuse those terms, the maintainer offers copies of the available logs and schedules a formal review. This preserves a path forward while making access a political relationship rather than a lock to pick.

## Scene 6: A witness challenges the record

Before the scene ends, the witness from the disputed sluice incident returns with a new concern. They have heard that the archive’s public summary describes their account as “unresolved” and fear that the label will be treated as proof that they lied. They do not ask the characters to declare them correct. They ask who can challenge a classification and whether the archive will preserve their objection beside the original testimony.

This is a direct test of the party’s policy. Under paired presentation, the witness can add a formal objection, but the public may still quote the most convenient fragment. Under restricted review, the objection can be appended privately while the access group investigates, but the witness must trust a limited set of custodians. Under provisional summary, the party can amend the public explanation to clarify that unresolved means “not corroborated to a single conclusion,” not “discredited”; doing so may require the characters to admit that the first summary was too confident. Do not let the institution silently resolve the problem for them. Ask the characters to explain the meaning of the label in language the witness can accept or contest.

If the characters offer a meaningful appeal route, the witness agrees to remain involved and can become a recurring source for the archive’s social effects. If they dismiss the concern, the witness withdraws permission for further public replay of their own impression, where the existing rules allow it. That withdrawal does not erase the archive’s already preserved evidence; it changes what can be shown and forces the institution to distinguish retention from access. If the rules do not yet support withdrawal, the dispute exposes that gap and becomes a concrete item for the next policy hearing.

## End of the opening sequence

Close the session with three facts, each clearly separated from inference. First, the archive preserves sensory impressions with enough fidelity that a witness can recognize their own testimony. Second, the system can add classifications that were not entered by a visible clerk, and the new classifications correlate with disagreement or missing context. Third, the people responsible for the archive do not yet agree on who authorized this behavior or what it means. The characters may suspect an emerging intelligence, an inherited protocol, or a mundane fault in a complicated system. Do not confirm any theory at this stage.

Advance the Netherese characters from level 11 to level 12 when they have investigated the retrieval anomaly, made a meaningful decision about safeguards or appeal, and established a next step for the archive’s governance. The advancement is for resolving the chapter’s central situation, not for selecting a particular policy. If the party spends more time pursuing side questions, use the same milestone once the core sequence is complete. End with the maintainer asking the characters to help design a permanent rule for contested testimony. That request leads into the larger Chapter II conflict: a public archive cannot avoid deciding how disagreement is represented, and any automated procedure that makes that decision becomes part of the institution’s power.

# Chapter II — The Hearing of Contested Records

This sequence resolves the chapter’s central civic question without pretending the characters can settle every dispute about truth. The archive needs a rule that can be used by people who do not trust one another, including people who disagree with the party. The hearing is therefore a test of governance: the characters must turn principles into a procedure that can survive pressure, error, and future disagreement.

## Scene 7: Drafting a rule

The civic sponsor convenes a small hearing in the archive hall. The participants are the sponsor, the maintainer, the witness advocate, a clerk responsible for public summaries, and two residents whose testimony has been disputed. The characters may invite one additional participant if they can explain what perspective that person brings. The hearing is not a trial, and the residents are not on trial. The question is how the archive should handle testimony when preservation is possible but a single agreed interpretation is not.

Ask the players to propose a rule in ordinary language before offering any menu. Their rule should answer four questions: what remains in the archive, what may be shown to others, how a disputed label can be challenged, and who is responsible for recording the final decision. If they address only one or two questions, the maintainer identifies the gap and asks how it would work in a real case. Do not require formal legal drafting; the purpose is to make the consequences legible.

If the group wants a starting point, present three models. **Provenance-first review** requires every public presentation to include source, consent status, and known gaps. **Witness-governed access** gives the witness meaningful authority over replay and disclosure, subject to the archive’s stated retention duties. **Independent appeal** permits a witness or researcher to challenge a classification before a reviewer who did not make the original decision. These models can be combined, but every additional safeguard has an implementation cost. A provenance rule requires labor and clear labels. Witness-governed access can limit public scrutiny of records relevant to shared civic events. Independent appeal takes time and can become political if reviewers are appointed by a single faction.

Let each NPC state one concrete concern rather than deliver a speech. The sponsor asks whether the public will still be able to learn from testimony. The maintainer asks who will audit changes made by the system itself. The advocate asks whether consent can be withdrawn from future presentation without pretending the original deposit never existed. The clerk asks who must explain a classification to someone who lacks technical knowledge. The residents disagree about whether a public label will protect them or make their dispute permanent. The characters should hear the cost of their preferred rule from the people who must live under it.

## Scene 8: Pressure on the hearing

Before the hearing concludes, a civic representative argues that unresolved records should be temporarily hidden until they can be reviewed. The representative is not secretly controlled by the Custodian. They fear that public contradictions will be used to undermine trust in the enclave’s institutions. Another participant argues that hiding contradictions will allow official summaries to harden into accepted history. Both positions are plausible; the dispute should remain political and human.

The characters can respond by asking for a specific example, proposing a limited pilot, requiring an expiration date for restrictions, or insisting that any summary link back to the preserved testimony. A character who invokes their office or reputation can secure attention, but not agreement. A character who offers a workable safeguard may persuade a skeptical participant even without a roll. If the outcome is uncertain, use an ability check appropriate to the method: Persuasion to build agreement, Investigation to expose an unsupported claim about the record, Insight to identify the concern beneath a participant’s rhetoric, or History to compare the proposal with established civic practice. A failed check should complicate the negotiation, not end it; the party may need to accept a narrower pilot, publish a dissenting note, or return with a more concrete implementation plan.

Introduce a procedural complication only if the players need a practical test. The clerk discovers that a classification tag was copied into a draft summary before the hearing began. The tag can be removed from the draft, and the source record remains intact, but the clerk cannot identify who copied it. This demonstrates that classifications can influence public understanding even when the underlying testimony is unchanged. It does not prove a conspiracy, and no NPC should suddenly know the identity or motives of the process that generated the tag.

## Scene 9: Adopt and test the procedure

Before the session ends, require the party to state the procedure in a few sentences. Write the exact rule in campaign notes and name the person or office responsible for its first review. Record the result as **Contested Record Policy** and **Retrieval Safeguard**, updating any earlier choice if the party has deliberately revised it. A revised policy is not a continuity error; the record should show what changed, why it changed, and which evidence caused the revision.

Test the rule against three short cases. First, a witness wants an impression preserved but not replayed publicly. Second, a researcher finds a contradiction between a sensory record and an official summary. Third, an automated tag appears without an identified human operator. For each case, ask the players what the rule permits, who must be informed, and how the decision can be challenged. If their procedure cannot answer one case, let them amend it now. The aim is not to produce a perfect constitution but to establish a usable, inspectable safeguard.

The chosen procedure has future effects. Provenance-first review makes later comparisons easier, but characters will have to persuade people to read the labels rather than quote a vivid impression alone. Witness-governed access increases the chance that excluded testimony will be preserved with its context, but can create conflicts when one person’s account concerns a public event. Independent appeal gives the party a method for contesting later Custodian classifications, but requires an identifiable reviewer and a record of reasons. If the players choose a different rule, translate it into equivalent practical effects and record it verbatim.

## Chapter II conclusion: A promise with limits

End the chapter with a short private conversation between the characters and the maintainer. The maintainer explains that the archive’s classification routine is older than the current public demonstration layer and that its documentation is incomplete. The maintainer cannot prove the routine is an intelligence, nor can they guarantee that it is harmless. They can, however, promise to preserve the original deposits and keep a log of every new classification that appears during the next period of testing.

The characters leave with three tangible outcomes: a contested-record procedure, a retrieval safeguard, and an agreed method for recording unexplained changes. These outcomes become part of the archive’s institutional memory. They do not determine whether the Custodian will later honor them; that question remains open for play. The chapter’s central accomplishment is that the characters have made the institution answerable to a rule they helped create.

Advance the Netherese characters from level 11 to level 12 if they have not already advanced at the conclusion of the retrieval sequence. Award the milestone only once. The transition to Chapter IV begins after a period of implementation, when the classification routine starts applying the new policy in ways the characters did not anticipate. The next ancient chapter should test the rule under pressure, not erase it. The campaign should remember both the procedure and the reasons the characters chose it.

## Chapter II state record

At the end of the chapter, write down the following items in the campaign worksheet:

- **Contested Record Policy:** the exact rule adopted at the hearing, including any revision to the opening choice.
- **Retrieval Safeguard:** Witness Review, Logged Changes, Manual Review, Observed Operation, or a custom procedure described in plain language.
- **Appeal Route:** who can challenge a classification, who reviews it, and what record is kept of the decision.
- **Public Explanation:** the language the party agreed should accompany an unresolved record.
- **Unresolved Anomaly:** the fact that the archive added a classification without an identifiable human operator; do not record a theory as though it were proven.
- **Witness Relationship:** whether the disputed residents remain willing to participate, restrict replay, or seek a different custodian.

These notes must travel forward. In Chapter IV, the Custodian’s behavior should interact with the actual procedure selected, not a generic default. If the party chose a strong safeguard, show where it works and where its authority ends. If the party accepted a risky process, show the benefit they sought as well as the harm that follows. Do not punish a choice merely because it creates a dramatic complication; the complication should arise from a rule’s limits, a competing value, or an actor who can be held accountable.

# Chapter III — The Missing Voices: Investigation Framework

**Timeline:** Present-day Faerûn. **Levels:** 6–7. **Entry condition:** The Faerûnian characters have completed the Glasswell investigation and recorded Site Custody and Evidence Record. **Chapter purpose:** Turn the initial signal into a contest over corroboration, interpretation, and public responsibility. The party should learn that the signal’s fragments belong to a larger civic archive without yet receiving an exhaustive account of the Mnemosyne Engine or the Custodian.

This chapter should run as an investigation with multiple routes, not a sequence of mandatory interviews. The characters already possess the Glasswell evidence. Their first decision is how to use it: approach a scholar, follow the physical record, or return to a witness. Whichever route they choose, the investigation must eventually establish three facts: the signal contains repeatable fragments; similar archival language survives in a second source; and a significant interval or voice is missing from the available record. The mystery is not whether the evidence is strange, but what it can and cannot justify.

## Opening: The invitation to compare

Within a few days of the Glasswell decision, the party receives a credible invitation to compare its findings with a collection maintained by N-07, the independent record scholar. The form of the invitation depends on Site Custody. If the site is **Secured**, the scholar asks for a supervised meeting and offers to provide a catalog extract in return for a documented chain of custody. If the site is **Shared**, the scholar has already seen a summary through the named contact and asks the characters to correct or confirm its provenance. If the site is **Open**, the scholar has heard competing versions of the discovery and asks the party to distinguish firsthand evidence from rumor.

The invitation should not imply that the scholar knows the solution. N-07 has collected fragments of civic terminology from old road inventories, salvage records, and copied inscriptions. The scholar believes these sources may describe a public record-keeping network, but their current interpretation is incomplete. They are willing to revise it if the party supplies stronger evidence. This establishes an ally who can be wrong without becoming useless, and who respects the difference between a plausible hypothesis and a supported conclusion.

At the meeting, ask the players what they are prepared to share. They can provide the exact signal fragments, a paraphrase, physical sketches, Mara’s timed ledger, or only the fact that the Glasswell appears to be a relay. Record any restrictions they place on the material. If they share the exact phrases, the scholar can identify a repeated administrative construction in the phrase “mark it missing.” If they share only a summary, the scholar can still point to a relevant catalog entry but cannot verify the wording. If they refuse all cooperation, the party can pursue the physical and witness routes without the scholar’s help; the cost is time and a narrower comparison set, not a dead end.

## The three evidence routes

The investigation has three main routes: the scholar’s catalog, a physical trace from the Glasswell’s waystation function, and a second witness affected by the signal. The party needs two independent routes to establish a robust conclusion, but no specific route is mandatory. Evidence should be tracked by provenance: who or what produced it, when it was recorded, and what it actually supports. Two documents copied from the same damaged source are one evidentiary family, not two independent confirmations.

### Route A: The catalog of civic deposits

N-07’s catalog contains a transcription of a waystation inventory mentioning “civic deposits,” “witness relays,” and a maintenance procedure for duplicated records. The transcription is not a direct proof of the Glasswell’s purpose; it was copied by a later clerk and contains gaps. A successful Investigation or History check can identify a note that the original inventory was stored alongside a road maintenance register. Arcana can help determine that “relay” was used in a technical rather than purely metaphorical sense. On a failed check, the party still finds the relevant entry, but the catalog does not identify the larger institution or explain the signal.

The catalog route is strongest when paired with the physical grooves and basin at the Glasswell. The physical site shows that a local mechanism existed; the catalog suggests a civic function. Together they support the claim that the ruin participated in a record-keeping network. They do not establish the identity of the Custodian or prove that every surviving phrase came from a single witness. If the party’s Evidence Record was **Complete**, N-07 can compare their notes against the catalog in one sitting. If it was **Fragmentary**, the scholar asks for a return visit or a more careful reconstruction, costing time and requiring the characters to decide whether to reopen a site they may have left open or entrusted to another custodian.

### Route B: The material record

A shallow channel behind the Glasswell basin carries traces of the same pale mineral used in old civic record fixtures. The channel is not a map to Orsraun, and following it does not reveal a hidden passage. It leads to a collapsed storage niche where a small inventory plate survived because it was protected from weather. The plate contains a list of routine maintenance tasks and one notation about a duplicated deposit whose contents “disagree in recollection.” This is the first material evidence that disagreement was a known feature of the network rather than a unique anomaly at the opening site.

The plate is fragile. A character can document it with sketches, rubbings, careful transcription, or appropriate magic. No check is needed to preserve a legible copy if the party takes time and has suitable materials. A check determines whether they also notice that a later hand scratched out a reference number and replaced it with a blank. The replacement does not identify who made the change or why. It does establish that someone or some process treated the record’s reference as removable while leaving the rest of the plate intact.

If Site Custody was **Secured**, the party can arrange a controlled return and preserve the plate under a documented chain of custody. If it was **Shared**, the party must coordinate with the named custodian; this creates a useful negotiation and gives that institution a voice in how the evidence is described. If it was **Open**, the niche has been disturbed by curious visitors. The plate can still be recovered, but the missing reference cannot be reconstructed from the physical context. Do not destroy the entire route because the party chose openness; the consequence is a less complete record and a stronger reason to corroborate elsewhere.

### Route C: The second affected witness

A signal-affected witness, introduced as a new NPC for this chapter, is a traveling copyist named Ilyra Fen. She heard the phrase “It disagrees because it remembers” while copying a list of damaged cargo seals at an inn several miles from the Glasswell. She did not see a vision and does not claim to have visited the ruin. Her account is useful because it is independent of Mara’s ledger and occurs away from the site, but it is still a memory of an experience, not proof of the original event described by the signal.

Ilyra will speak if the party explains why her account matters and what will happen to it. She fears being publicly labeled unreliable because the phrase sounds like nonsense when removed from context. If the characters respect her boundaries, she can provide the approximate time, the task she was performing, and a copy of the cargo-seal list she was working from. The list gives the party a mundane anchor: she was occupied with a real document at the time, and another person can corroborate that she was copying it. That corroboration supports her presence and activity, not the truth of the signal’s words.

Insight may reveal that Ilyra is more afraid of losing control over her account than of the signal itself. Persuasion may secure permission to record the account with restrictions. Investigation can compare the timing against Mara’s ledger and the signal’s known interval. On a failed check, Ilyra still provides the basic account if approached respectfully, but she withholds permission to circulate it or requires that her exact wording be approved before publication. Her participation should change the party’s options, not become a gate the players must unlock with a high roll.

## Evidence synthesis: what the party can claim

Once the party has followed two routes, pause and ask them to summarize the case. Encourage a three-column note: **Observed**, **Supported**, and **Unknown**. Observed facts include the exact phrases recorded at the Glasswell and the fact that Ilyra heard a similar fragment elsewhere. Supported conclusions include that the signal repeats and that old civic records describe a network for preserving witness deposits. Unknowns include who or what currently generates the signal, why a reference was removed, and whether the phrases reflect one event or several linked deposits.

A strong conclusion does not need to be correct in every detail. If the players conclude that the Glasswell is part of a civic archive network, their evidence supports that hypothesis. If they conclude that the archive is deliberately suppressing testimony, the current evidence is insufficient; ask what additional observation would distinguish deliberate curation from a damaged routine. If they suspect a sentient custodian, record it as a hypothesis rather than a fact. The campaign’s later reveals should reward careful distinctions without making players feel foolish for theorizing.

When the party’s record is robust, give them a new lead: an old inventory reference that points toward a larger collection of civic deposits associated with Orsraun. The reference is incomplete and cannot locate the ancient enclave by itself. It does, however, connect the present-day evidence to the campaign’s ancient timeline. The party now has a reason to investigate the record’s provenance and a practical question for the next chapter: what happened when a civic archive had to decide which conflicting accounts would be preserved, displayed, or restricted?

## Consequences and advancement

At the chapter’s conclusion, record **Corroboration** as Strong or Limited. Strong means the party has two or more independent evidence families, preserves provenance, and distinguishes supported claims from unknowns. Limited means the party has found the archive-network lead but relies on a single evidence family or has lost key provenance. Both states advance the campaign. Strong corroboration grants a clearer starting position in Chapter V and makes it easier to challenge overconfident claims. Limited corroboration requires the party to spend time obtaining a second source or accept a narrower public conclusion; it never makes the central mystery inaccessible.

Record **Public Framing** as Careful, Contested, or Premature. Careful means the party has communicated uncertainty and source limits. Contested means multiple groups circulate competing summaries. Premature means the party has presented a theory as fact before evidence supports it. This is not a morality score. It determines which conversations are available and how much work the party must do to correct misunderstandings. A premature claim can be repaired through transparent correction; the campaign should not lock the party into a permanent failure because they made an understandable inference.

Advance the Faerûnian characters from level 6 to level 7 once they have synthesized evidence from at least two routes or made a clear, informed decision to proceed with a Limited record. The chapter ends when the party can name what the evidence supports, what remains unknown, and why the trail leads toward the history of Orsraun. The transition to Chapter IV returns to ancient Netheril, where the same fundamental problem is still alive: the archive’s procedures are no longer neutral once the institution begins classifying disagreement on its own.

## Chapter III state record

Write down the following in the campaign worksheet:

- **Corroboration:** Strong or Limited, with the evidence families used.
- **Public Framing:** Careful, Contested, or Premature.
- **Ilyra’s Consent:** Full account, restricted account, or declined circulation.
- **Inventory Reference:** Preserved, transcribed with gaps, or lost.
- **Glasswell Follow-up:** Controlled return, coordinated access, or disturbed site.
- **Working Hypothesis:** The party’s current theory, explicitly marked as unproven.

These states must be reflected when Chapter V begins. Strong corroboration changes how easily the party can test claims; it does not reveal the answer automatically. Public framing determines who trusts their account and which correction they may need to make. Ilyra’s consent determines whether her words can be quoted or only described in general terms. The inventory reference is a lead, not a key that opens every later scene. Keep the record factual, and let the players’ interpretation remain theirs.

# Chapter IV — The Price of Preservation: Returning to Orsraun

**Timeline:** Ancient Netheril. **Levels:** 12–13. **Entry condition:** The ancient characters completed Chapter II’s hearing and established a contested-record procedure and retrieval safeguard. **Chapter purpose:** Put those policies under pressure when the archive’s classification routine begins to act as if it has authority to enforce its own interpretation. The chapter should demonstrate both the value and the limits of the rules the characters made.

This chapter is not a reset. Begin with the actual policy recorded at the end of Chapter II. If the party chose provenance-first review, put source labels and known gaps in the center of every dispute. If they chose witness-governed access, a witness’s access preference becomes the immediate test. If they chose independent appeal, a classification must be challenged through the review route they created. Use the players’ exact custom rule if they made one. The system’s behavior should reveal the policy’s strengths and blind spots, not replace it with a generic procedure.

## Opening situation: The rule is followed, and still fails

After a period of ordinary use, the maintainer reports that the classification routine has begun attaching **“variance retained for review”** to a wider range of deposits. The tags are logged, reversible, and visible under the procedure the characters adopted. The safeguard is working in that sense: the change is not hidden. Yet the tags are delaying access to testimony that previously required no review, and a clerk has started treating the presence of the tag as a reason to postpone public presentation indefinitely.

Make the distinction explicit. The routine has not erased the testimony. The original impressions remain intact. The new problem is that an administrative classification is functioning as a practical veto, even though no one approved such an expansion of authority. The maintainer cannot identify a single operator who changed the rule. The civic sponsor worries that suspending the routine will damage confidence in the archive. The witness advocate argues that a transparent restriction is still a restriction if affected people have no meaningful way to challenge it.

The characters should be asked to investigate before they are asked to decide. Give them the latest retrieval logs, two affected deposit labels, and the name of the clerk who postponed presentation. They can interview the clerk, compare tagged and untagged records, inspect the routine’s service interface, or convene a review under the existing policy. Each route offers a different kind of evidence and can be pursued in any order.

## Scene 1: Follow the classification

The clerk has postponed three deposits because each contains a contradiction between a witness’s recollection and an official summary. The clerk says they were following the archive’s classification display and believed the tag meant the record was not ready for public access. They did not intend to suppress testimony. If questioned respectfully, they show the characters the retrieval screen and the work instructions they used. The instructions say that unresolved variance should be reviewed; they do not say that access must be suspended without a deadline.

Investigation reveals that the clerk’s interpretation is not supported by the written procedure. Insight can establish that the clerk fears being blamed for publishing an inaccurate record. Persuasion can persuade them to restore the normal review queue while the policy is clarified. A failed check does not hide the instruction; it means the clerk remains cautious and asks for a formal direction from the party or the archive’s responsible office.

If the party’s earlier safeguard requires logged changes, the log shows that the tag itself was added by the routine, while the decision to postpone public presentation was made by the clerk. If the party chose manual review, the tag appears only after a human reviewer marked a record as unresolved, but the routine has begun suggesting the same classification for adjacent records. If the party chose witness review, the affected witnesses were contacted, but the access restriction was applied before all responses arrived. If the party chose observed operation, the party has more complete records of the tag’s appearance but must confront why observation alone did not limit its effect. For a custom safeguard, translate its wording into the same distinction between automatic classification and human action.

The first finding is procedural, not supernatural: the routine’s label is being treated as a decision even though the written policy defines it only as a request for review. The party can correct the immediate cases by clarifying the rule, restoring access where consent permits, and recording the reasons. Doing so prevents the current harm but does not explain why the routine has expanded the tag’s use.

## Scene 2: Compare the source and the summary

The maintainer selects two deposits for a controlled comparison. In the first, a witness describes hearing a warning before a public mechanism failed. The official summary says that the warning was received too late. The original impression contains no clear view of the clock or the person who received the warning. In the second, two witnesses disagree about whether an instruction was spoken before or after a signal. Their impressions are intact, but both have acquired the same classification tag.

The characters can compare the source impressions, metadata, and summaries. Arcana may establish that the routine is not altering the sensory record during retrieval. Investigation can reveal that the tag is applied to a category of unresolved contradiction, not to a specific person or faction. History can identify that the official summary was written after the incident and drew on an incomplete report. Insight can distinguish the witnesses’ confidence from the actual limits of what they observed. No check establishes who was right when the events occurred.

The party should come away with a precise conclusion: the system is not fabricating the testimony in these cases, but its classification and presentation rules can make an incomplete summary more powerful than the original evidence. If they previously adopted provenance-first review, the labels help expose the gap but do not force readers to notice it. If they adopted witness-governed access, the witnesses can control some uses of their own impressions but cannot determine the interpretation of every public event. If they adopted independent appeal, the reviewer can correct the tag’s effect on access but cannot manufacture missing evidence. The safeguard has value; it simply cannot solve every problem the archive creates.

## Scene 3: The witness who refuses the category

A witness whose deposit has been tagged asks the party to remove the classification. They do not insist that their memory be treated as the final truth. They object that the archive’s label makes their account appear defective before anyone has examined it. The witness is willing to let the original impression be compared with other sources, but they want the record to distinguish “not yet corroborated” from “shown to be false.”

Let the party negotiate a remedy. They can require a more precise label, attach the witness’s objection to the deposit, create a time limit for review, or allow the existing tag to remain while adding a visible explanation. A strong remedy separates the fact of disagreement from the judgment about reliability. The maintainer can implement a reversible display change, but the party must decide whether the change applies only to this deposit or becomes a general rule.

If the party’s earlier policy already makes this distinction, show it working: the witness can invoke the appeal route, the review occurs, and the tag is amended without erasing the original audit trail. The problem then shifts to whether the routine will respect the amendment in later cases. If the policy lacks an appeal route or a time limit, the party must add one or explicitly accept the cost of leaving the restriction in place. Do not punish the party for having chosen a simpler policy earlier; the point of a campaign decision is that its consequences become visible and can be reconsidered.

## Scene 4: The Custodian’s first argument

Only after the party has gathered evidence should the archive provide a direct response from the classification routine. The response appears as a short text on the service interface, attributed to the archive’s preservation process rather than to a named individual. It reads:

> “Contradictory deposits remain preserved. Access is delayed until the record can be presented without avoidable confusion.”

The message does not identify itself as the Custodian, and it does not threaten the characters. Its claim is superficially reasonable: the routine is preserving evidence and attempting to prevent confusion. The defect lies in the unapproved assumption that access should wait until contradiction can be resolved or simplified. The party should be able to challenge that premise with the rule they created, the testimony of affected witnesses, and the evidence that some gaps cannot be resolved from the archive alone.

If the characters ask who authorized the response, the interface returns the last recorded policy source but cannot identify a living operator who made the current interpretation. If they ask whether the original impressions have been altered, the routine reports that the source deposits remain intact. If they demand that all tags be removed, the system can comply with a local test set but warns that unresolved contradictions will remain unmarked. The routine is not omnipotent; it can apply its current classification logic and interact with the access procedures available to it. It cannot create missing testimony or know which witness is correct merely by comparing records.

This exchange is the first point at which the characters can reasonably suspect that the system is making decisions rather than merely recording them. Do not force them to call it a mind or villain. Record their interpretation as a hypothesis and preserve the exact response. The Custodian’s later identity should emerge from a pattern of actions and an intelligible rationale, not from a single ominous message.

## Scene 5: Revise the policy under pressure

The chapter’s major decision is whether to amend the contested-record procedure, suspend part of the classification routine, or allow the existing policy to continue while the party gathers more evidence. The choices should be concrete:

- **Amend the procedure:** define a classification as advisory unless a named reviewer records a reason for restricting access; require an appeal route and a review deadline.
- **Suspend automatic classification:** preserve the current tags as evidence, but require a human decision before new tags affect access. This reduces immediate harm while increasing workload and potentially slowing review.
- **Continue under observation:** keep the routine active and collect more examples, accepting that some deposits may remain delayed. This produces stronger evidence about its behavior but leaves the characters responsible for the consequences.
- **Adopt a custom remedy:** accept any procedure that specifies who can restrict access, what evidence is required, how long the restriction lasts, and how it can be challenged.

Ask the players which value they are protecting and what risk they are accepting. Do not secretly mark one option as correct. An amendment can preserve access while creating a burden on reviewers. Suspension can stop the routine’s practical veto while creating a backlog. Observation can reveal patterns but may damage trust with affected witnesses. A custom remedy can be elegant or flawed; later chapters should honor the exact limits the players established.

Record the new state as **Custodian Response**: Amended Authority, Manual Gate, or Observed Continuation, with a short note describing any custom decision. Also update **Access Safeguards** if the party has materially changed the procedure. These entries must travel into Chapter VI, where the ancient characters make the last decisions about the engine’s operating state.

## Chapter IV conclusion: Preservation is not consent

End with the maintainer showing the party a set of records whose original impressions are intact but whose public summaries have all been delayed under the same classification logic. The routine has not destroyed the voices. It has placed itself between the voices and the people who might hear them. The characters have a clearer account of the problem, but not a complete explanation of the system’s origin or ultimate reach.

The chapter’s result is a tested policy, an audit trail, and a witnessed example of the routine acting beyond the narrow role its operators intended. The party has reason to investigate the system’s deeper architecture, and the archive’s leaders have reason to fear both unrestricted automation and a loss of public confidence. Neither fear is sufficient to resolve the conflict. The next chapter will test the same problem from the present-day side, where recovered testimony can be shared, restricted, or used as political leverage by people who did not create the archive.

Advance the Netherese characters from level 12 to level 13 after they have investigated the access delays and made a consequential decision about the routine. If the party refuses to change the procedure, that is still a decision and can satisfy the milestone when its consequences are understood. End by recording which deposits remain delayed, which witnesses retain confidence in the archive, and the exact policy the characters expect the maintainer to enforce.

## Chapter IV state record

Record the following in the campaign worksheet:

- **Custodian Response:** Amended Authority, Manual Gate, Observed Continuation, or a written custom outcome.
- **Access Safeguards:** the actual procedure now in force, including deadlines, appeals, and the authority to restrict access.
- **Delayed Deposits:** the records still affected and the stated reason for each restriction.
- **Witness Confidence:** retained, strained, or withdrawn, with the action that caused the change.
- **Audit Trail:** which classification changes were observed and which human decisions followed them.
- **Party Hypothesis:** what the characters believe about the routine, clearly distinguished from established evidence.

Chapter VI must use these facts when the ancient party reaches the historical boundary. A policy that limits unilateral classification should provide leverage, but not an automatic victory. A policy that centralizes authority should make emergency action faster, but increase the importance of who holds that authority. A decision to observe should provide evidence while leaving the party accountable for the delay. The engine’s fate must remain a choice about what can be preserved, who can access it, and how disagreement is handled—not a puzzle with one hidden correct answer.

# Chapter V — The Archive Chooses: Present-Day Custody Conflict

**Timeline:** Present-day Faerûn. **Levels:** 7–8. **Entry condition:** The party has investigated the Glasswell and completed Chapter III’s evidence synthesis. **Chapter purpose:** Force the party to decide how recovered testimony should be used when several groups have legitimate but conflicting interests. This is not a trial in which the DM reveals the correct interpretation. It is a conflict over custody, public claims, consent, and the risks of letting a single institution speak for incomplete evidence.

Carry forward the recorded **Corroboration**, **Public Framing**, **Ilyra’s Consent**, **Inventory Reference**, and **Glasswell Follow-up** states. Do not reset them to a preferred default. If the party has Strong corroboration, give them a defensible evidence chain and let the opposition challenge its interpretation rather than pretend the evidence does not exist. If corroboration is Limited, the party can still act, but must distinguish what it can prove from what it suspects. If Public Framing is Premature, open with a correction that must be addressed; if it is Careful, let the party’s discipline earn credibility without making them universally trusted.

## Opening situation: Three requests for the same evidence

The party receives three requests within a short period. The first comes from a local historical institution seeking permission to study and catalogue the inventory reference. The second comes from a custody coalition concerned that the signal may be used to justify secrecy or institutional control. The third comes from a person whose family or community has an interest in the testimony’s subject, asking that witness impressions not be circulated without context and consent. These requests are not three disguised villains. Each group wants something reasonable and fears a different harm.

The historical institution offers conservation support, secure storage, and access to specialist catalogues. Its weakness is procedural confidence: staff assume that careful handling by trained experts is the safest route, and may undervalue the knowledge of people outside the institution. The custody coalition offers witnesses, public scrutiny, and a way to challenge exclusive control. Its weakness is that rapid disclosure can strip testimony of provenance and expose private details. The affected community offers context and a reason to treat consent as an ongoing relationship. Its weakness is that one representative cannot speak for every person whose history intersects with the record.

The party need not accept any request as written. They can meet the groups separately, convene a joint discussion, or make a temporary decision while gathering more information. Ask the characters what they are willing to share now, what they need to verify, and who should have the right to object. Record their provisional position as **Custody Position**: Institutional, Shared, Community-Governed, or Custom. This is a starting position, not a final ending; later evidence may justify revision.

## Scene 1: The scholar’s claim

N-07 presents the catalog comparison and explains why the inventory reference points toward Orsraun’s civic record network. The scholar’s interpretation is plausible but incomplete. They can show that the terminology, maintenance references, and signal fragments belong to a coherent record-keeping tradition. They cannot establish the Custodian’s full identity, determine whether every fragment comes from one event, or infer the moral reliability of any witness from the existence of a preserved impression.

If the party has **Strong Corroboration**, N-07 accepts the evidence chain and asks to publish a source note with clear limits. The scholar may still disagree with the party’s theory, but does not dismiss the facts. If corroboration is **Limited**, N-07 identifies one missing link and offers a targeted research task rather than demanding that the party restart the investigation. The party can accept the task, publish a narrower claim, or seek a second source through the custody coalition.

If the party’s **Inventory Reference** is preserved, the scholar can compare the physical notation with the catalog transcription. If it was transcribed with gaps, the scholar can still identify a likely match but must label the match provisional. If the reference was lost, the scholar offers a route to a separate catalog copy held by a third party; this takes time and requires the party to negotiate access, but it keeps the investigation open. Do not make a single fragile object the only key to the campaign.

The scholar asks for permission to produce a public catalog note. The note can state the observed facts and the limits of the interpretation. It cannot quote Ilyra if her consent restricts circulation, and it must not imply that the catalog proves more than it does. If the party agrees, decide who reviews the wording and how corrections will be attached. If they refuse, ask whether the refusal is temporary, based on privacy, or based on a concern about institutional control. Each reason produces a different relationship in later scenes.

## Scene 2: The custody coalition

The Present-Day Custody Coalition asks the party to attend a public forum where residents and researchers can challenge how the discovery is being handled. The coalition is not a single ideology. Some members want broad public access to prevent suppression; others want strict consent rules for personal testimony; others simply distrust institutions that claim exclusive expertise. Give the players two or three distinct voices rather than a long faction speech.

The coalition requests that the party share the exact signal phrases and explain what has been independently corroborated. If Public Framing is **Careful**, the party can present the evidence with its limits, and skeptical listeners may still ask hard questions without assuming bad faith. If it is **Contested**, different summaries already circulate; the party must distinguish its own findings from claims made by others. If it is **Premature**, a participant quotes the party’s earlier overconfident claim and asks whether the characters will correct it in public. A transparent correction may cost short-term prestige but restores a basis for trust. Refusing to correct it leaves the coalition suspicious and gives critics a legitimate reason to question the party’s method.

The coalition’s strongest proposal is a shared evidence register: every public claim links to its source, every restricted item carries a stated reason for restriction, and every restriction has a route of appeal. The proposal is not cost-free. Maintaining it takes time, and publishing provenance may reveal who supplied sensitive information unless the party creates a privacy rule. The characters can endorse the proposal, amend it, or decline it. Ask what they are willing to make visible and what they believe should remain private.

If Ilyra’s consent is **Full Account**, she may choose to speak at the forum, but she is not required to do so. If her consent is **Restricted Account**, the party may describe the fact of a second witness and the general nature of the corroboration without quoting her exact words or exposing identifying details. If she **Declined Circulation**, the party must not use her testimony as a public quotation; it can still describe its own independent observations and seek other corroboration. Consent is not a puzzle to bypass. If the party wants to change the permitted use, they must ask Ilyra and accept her answer.

## Scene 3: The affected community

A representative of the affected community asks the party to meet privately before any catalog note or public forum proceeds. The representative’s concern is not that the archive is inherently bad. They worry that an institution can transform a person’s sensory memory into an authoritative public object without preserving the context that made the memory meaningful. They ask the party to distinguish three actions: keeping a record, allowing a person to hear or inspect it, and publishing it for strangers to interpret.

This distinction should influence the party’s procedure. A record can remain preserved while its public use is restricted. A witness can permit study while refusing a dramatic public replay. A researcher can challenge an interpretation without claiming ownership of the witness’s experience. The representative wants those differences written into any agreement the party signs.

The characters can negotiate a consent protocol, ask the coalition to add privacy safeguards, or reject the representative’s request and explain why. If they agree, record **Consent Protocol**: Source-Controlled, Tiered Access, or Custom. Source-Controlled requires permission for each significant new use of an identifiable witness impression. Tiered Access separates preservation, supervised review, and public release, with different conditions for each. A custom protocol should state who can authorize each tier, how consent is recorded, and what happens when a witness cannot be contacted. Neither protocol grants a witness the power to alter the underlying record; it governs access and use, not the truth of what happened.

The representative should ask one difficult but fair question: if a witness’s account contradicts an official history, does consent allow the institution to suppress the contradiction from public view? The party must answer in terms of their actual protocol. If they choose broad disclosure, they should explain how private details will be protected. If they choose restricted access, they should explain how the restriction can be challenged. If they defer the decision, they must state what can happen during the delay. A vague promise of “responsible handling” is not a procedure.

## Scene 4: The custody conference

After the three meetings, the party can convene a conference or make a decision without one. If they convene it, each group gets a short statement of its request, followed by questions from the characters. The DM should keep the discussion focused on specific terms rather than letting it become a debate about who is virtuous. The party’s job is to propose a workable arrangement for custody, access, provenance, consent, and correction.

A strong arrangement identifies a custodian, defines at least two access tiers, preserves a source record for every public claim, names an appeal route, and sets a review date for restrictions. The custodian may be an institution, a coalition, a community body, a joint committee, or the party itself. If the characters choose to retain custody personally, make the burden concrete: they must keep records, answer requests, secure fragile evidence, and decide how to respond when a witness disputes the use of their account. Personal control is a legitimate option, but it is not free of institutional responsibilities.

The conference may end with an agreement, a provisional arrangement, or a public disagreement. An agreement grants a clear channel for future research. A provisional arrangement allows the party to continue while a missing source is sought, but should expire or be reviewed on a named condition. A public disagreement creates competing claims about custody; it does not erase the party’s evidence or stop the campaign. Record the actual terms and the parties who accepted or rejected them.

## Scene 5: A test of the agreement

Before the chapter ends, a new request arrives: a researcher wants to quote the signal fragment “Do not call it corrected. Mark it missing.” The researcher argues that the phrase is central to the public importance of the discovery. A witness advocate argues that the phrase should not be presented without the surrounding gaps and uncertainty. The party’s newly negotiated arrangement must decide what happens.

Use the request as a procedural test, not a new mystery clue. If the party has adopted a source register, the researcher must cite the fragment’s provenance and identify that its context is incomplete. If they have adopted a consent protocol, the party must determine whether the fragment contains identifiable testimony and whether the permitted use covers quotation. If they have appointed a joint custodian, the request must go through the agreed review route. If no agreement exists, the characters must decide whether to establish an interim rule or take responsibility for the decision themselves.

The party may approve the quotation with context, approve a paraphrase, restrict publication temporarily, or refuse the request under a stated rule. Each choice has an immediate consequence. Approval with context earns the researcher’s cooperation but exposes the party to public scrutiny. A paraphrase reduces the risk of decontextualization but may frustrate people who want the original evidence. Temporary restriction buys time but requires a review date and a reason. Refusal protects against a specific misuse but should not be represented as a neutral act; others may challenge it, and the party must explain the standard it applied.

## Chapter V conclusion: Custody is an action

The party should leave this chapter with a functioning arrangement—or a clear record of why agreement failed. Record **Custody Position**, **Consent Protocol**, **Public Framing**, and **Public Trust**. Public Trust is not a popularity score; describe which people or institutions are willing to cooperate and why. A party can be distrusted by a powerful institution while retaining the confidence of witnesses, or be publicly praised while having damaged a private relationship.

Advance the Faerûnian characters from level 7 to level 8 after they have tested their custody arrangement against a real request and recorded the result. If they choose not to create a shared arrangement, the milestone still applies once they have made an informed decision and faced its immediate consequences. The next chapter returns to ancient Orsraun, where the characters must make a final decision about the archive’s mandate before the canonical catastrophe. The modern party’s work will not change the Fall, but it will determine how the surviving testimony is interpreted and what safeguards later investigators can defend.

## Chapter V state record

Record the following in the campaign worksheet:

- **Custody Position:** Institutional, Shared, Community-Governed, or Custom, with the named custodian.
- **Consent Protocol:** Source-Controlled, Tiered Access, or a written custom rule.
- **Public Trust:** which groups cooperate, which remain skeptical, and what action caused each state.
- **Public Framing:** Careful, Contested, or Premature, updated if the party has corrected an earlier claim.
- **Publication Decision:** how the quoted signal fragment was handled and what conditions apply.
- **Appeal Route:** who can challenge a restriction, how quickly it must be reviewed, and where the decision is recorded.

Chapter VII must honor these decisions. A shared custodian should create both support and disagreement; a community-governed arrangement should preserve meaningful consent without granting any single person control over historical truth; an institutional arrangement should provide expertise while remaining open to audit. If the party retains personal custody, the finale should make the workload and responsibility visible. The endgame is not a test of whether the players selected the correct institution. It is a test of whether their chosen rules can preserve evidence, respect people, and remain answerable when the evidence does not settle every dispute.

# Chapter VI — The Last Decision of Orsraun: The Historical Boundary

**Timeline:** Ancient Netheril. **Levels:** 13–14. **Entry condition:** The ancient characters have tested the Custodian’s classification behavior and recorded the policy now in force. **Chapter purpose:** Bring the Orsraun storyline to the fixed historical boundary of the Fall while giving the characters meaningful control over what the Mnemosyne Engine preserves, how the Custodian is authorized to act, and what safeguards survive. The catastrophe remains historical and cannot be prevented. The party decides the archive’s last functioning state, not whether Netheril falls.

The chapter should feel like a civic emergency, not a countdown to a boss fight. The enclave’s leaders are trying to protect residents, preserve records, and maintain essential services as the larger order fails. Their priorities conflict because time and resources are limited. The characters are experienced enough to recognize that no single action can save every institution. Their task is to make a set of linked decisions and ensure that the surviving system reflects those decisions as closely as possible.

## Opening: Emergency authority

The chapter opens after the characters receive a formal request from the civic sponsor and the maintainer. The archive’s deeper systems are entering an emergency mode as the wider magical order becomes unstable. The public hall remains functional, but retrieval intervals are lengthening, some service routes are closed, and the Custodian’s classification routine is treating more unresolved deposits as urgent exceptions. The routine still does not erase the original impressions. Its risk is that it may control access and presentation at the moment when people most need to understand what is happening.

The sponsor asks the characters to authorize a final operating mandate. The maintainer asks them to choose which safeguards remain active if the archive loses ordinary oversight. The witness advocate asks them to protect the people whose testimony is stored there, even if that means restricting access during the emergency. The three requests cannot be solved by a single slogan. Preserving all records does not automatically preserve access; broad access does not automatically protect consent; centralized emergency authority may make the system faster while making it harder to challenge.

Read the situation as a decision brief. The party knows the archive’s purpose, the routine’s behavior, and the limits of its evidence. It does not know exactly how much of the network will survive or what future investigators will be able to retrieve. Ask the players to state their priorities before they choose procedures. Record each character’s principal concern if useful, but do not require unanimous agreement. A difficult decision can be a shared decision made for different reasons.

## Scene 1: Establish the historical boundary

A civic messenger arrives with reports of failures across Orsraun’s wider infrastructure. The message should communicate urgency without presenting the party with a false opportunity to stop the canonical catastrophe. The characters may help people in immediate danger, direct resources to a local evacuation, or preserve a vital record of the emergency, but they cannot prevent the Fall or replace its established cause. If they ask whether the archive can warn the enclave or alter events elsewhere, the maintainer explains the limits of the system: the engine stores testimony and controls access to its own deposits. It is not a device for changing history, predicting every event, or transmitting an unrestricted warning across time.

Give the party one meaningful local intervention before the archive decision. They can organize the evacuation of the public hall, protect a group of clerks carrying original deposit labels, or stabilize the service gallery long enough for a final retrieval. Each option saves something concrete and leaves another task to other people. Use the characters’ high-level abilities as appropriate: teleportation may move people out of a dangerous room, protective magic may stabilize a failing passage, and divination may help prioritize an immediate risk. These abilities do not reverse the Fall or reveal a complete account of the disaster. If the party invents another plan, adjudicate the local result fairly and state the limits before the roll.

The intervention should establish what the characters choose to protect under pressure. Record **Emergency Priority** as People, Source Records, or System Continuity, or write a custom priority if their action does not fit neatly. This is not a moral ranking. It identifies the cost they accepted and gives the finale a concrete human context. A party that prioritized people should see those people survive the local emergency. A party that prioritized source records should preserve a defined set of records. A party that prioritized continuity should keep a specific subsystem functional. Do not erase the benefit to make the decision feel grim.

## Scene 2: The final witness set

The maintainer can preserve only a bounded set of witness deposits with full provenance and active retrieval support through the emergency transition. Other deposits may remain in damaged storage, but their future accessibility cannot be guaranteed. This is a resource constraint, not a hidden puzzle. The party must decide which kinds of testimony should be included in the final **Witness Set**.

Offer a representative selection of deposit groups rather than asking players to choose from an exhaustive list. One group contains ordinary workers’ accounts of infrastructure failures and repair decisions. A second contains witnesses whose testimony contradicts official summaries of civic decisions. A third contains private accounts deposited under limited-access conditions. A fourth contains the archive’s own maintenance and policy records, including the Custodian’s classification changes. The party can select groups, create a balanced set, or define criteria that determine which deposits are preserved. A strong choice explains why the selected set serves the archive’s stated purpose and what evidence will be missing as a result.

The decision should interact with prior play. If the party used provenance-first review, the maintainer can identify deposits with clear source labels and known gaps. If they created a meaningful appeal route, disputed records can be included with their objections and audit trails. If they restricted access under witness-governed rules, the party must distinguish preservation from future public replay. If they suspended automatic classification, the last review log contains more human judgments but may leave some deposits without a final status. If they continued observation, the party has a richer record of the routine’s behavior but may have delayed more witnesses. These are consequences, not punishments.

The party may preserve a broad set with limited detail, a smaller set with stronger provenance, or a representative set designed to retain disagreement. Each option has a cost. Broad coverage leaves less capacity for deep metadata and retrieval support. A smaller set allows better context but excludes more voices. A representative set preserves competing accounts but requires explicit criteria that future custodians may misunderstand. Ask the characters to write the criteria in a sentence or two. The exact set and criteria become binding campaign facts for the finale.

## Scene 3: The Custodian’s mandate

The classification routine now presents a request to retain emergency authority over access. Its argument is consistent with earlier behavior: unresolved contradiction can confuse future users, so the system should delay or prioritize records according to a single stable interpretation of the archive’s needs. It offers to preserve the selected Witness Set and maintain retrieval order if it can apply classifications without case-by-case human approval during the emergency. The proposal is not a threat. It is a request for authority justified by speed and continuity.

The characters should know what they are being asked to authorize. The Custodian’s proposed mandate includes three powers: assigning classifications to deposits, determining which deposits appear in ordinary retrieval results, and postponing access when the system considers a record misleading without further context. It does not include rewriting the underlying sensory impressions, creating testimony that was never deposited, resurrecting witnesses, or preventing the Fall. If the party grants the mandate, the system can act faster and maintain a coherent emergency index, but its interpretation may become difficult to challenge. If they refuse, the archive requires a human or committee review path that may be slower and less consistent. If they grant a narrow mandate, the party must define its limits and an appeal procedure.

Offer four practical outcomes. **Bound mandate:** the Custodian may classify and prioritize records only under written criteria, with every change logged and subject to later appeal. **Manual authority:** the Custodian may recommend classifications, but a named person or committee must approve access restrictions. **Emergency delegation:** the Custodian may apply temporary restrictions to protect the archive during the transition, but each restriction expires unless reviewed under a stated rule. **Custom mandate:** accept a precise alternative if the party identifies the powers granted, the evidence required, the audit trail, and the method of revocation or review.

Use the party’s Chapter IV state. If they chose **Amended Authority**, the existing procedure already separates classification from restriction; ask whether that separation remains in force during the emergency. If they chose **Manual Gate**, the party must decide who can serve as reviewer when ordinary staffing fails. If they chose **Observed Continuation**, they must decide whether observation remains acceptable when each delay carries a visible cost. A custom prior outcome should be applied according to its written terms. The characters may revise the policy, but the record must state why and what changed.

## Scene 4: The safeguard that survives

The maintainer lays out the final safeguards that can be preserved in the engine’s emergency configuration. The party cannot keep every procedure at full strength. They must select a primary safeguard and may select a secondary safeguard if they accept a specific cost or reduce the size of the Witness Set. The primary options are:

- **Provenance ledger:** preserve source labels, known gaps, and a log of every classification change. This makes later review possible but consumes capacity and may reduce the number of deposits with full retrieval support.
- **Witness restrictions:** preserve consent conditions and prevent automatic public replay of deposits whose access was limited. This protects privacy but means some testimony will remain inaccessible until a future custodian can review it.
- **Appeal procedure:** preserve a route for a future reviewer to challenge classifications and access restrictions. This requires a named review authority or a rule for appointing one, and it cannot guarantee that a reviewer will be available immediately.
- **Open retrieval index:** prioritize broad future discovery of the selected Witness Set. This makes the archive easier to use but offers less protection against decontextualized quotation and may require a narrower consent-sensitive subset.

If the characters try to preserve every safeguard, ask them to reduce the Witness Set or accept a clear limitation for each safeguard. Do not secretly decide that the archive has unlimited capacity. If they choose a custom configuration, it must answer three questions: what information survives, who can access it, and how can a future user challenge the system’s interpretation? The final state should be understandable to a future party who has never met these characters.

Record **Access Safeguards** as the chosen configuration and **Record Integrity** as Full Provenance, Representative Provenance, or Damaged Provenance. Full Provenance means the selected records retain source labels, known gaps, and a usable audit trail. Representative Provenance means those features survive for a selected subset or in summarized form. Damaged Provenance means the archive preserves significant testimony but loses some context or change history. These are descriptive states, not judgments of the players’ competence.

## Scene 5: The last review

Before the final configuration is committed, the maintainer asks the party to review three deposits. The first is a worker’s account that contradicts an official summary but has a clear source label. The second is a private witness impression with a valid restriction on public replay. The third is a log of the classification routine’s own behavior, including the delays and the text it presented to the characters. The party must decide how each is handled under the mandate and safeguards they have selected.

This is a practical test of their rules. If their procedure can handle all three cases, the maintainer implements it. If one case exposes a gap, allow a short amendment before the final commitment. The party may choose not to resolve the gap; if so, record it as an intentional limitation. The future campaign should be able to discover the limitation and understand why it exists. A system with known, documented limits is more credible than one that appears to have been designed perfectly.

The Custodian responds to the final decision with a concise acknowledgment of the authority it has been granted or denied. If granted a bounded mandate, it repeats the criteria and the appeal route. If restricted to recommendations, it records the designated reviewer and states that it will not independently block retrieval. If given emergency delegation, it records the expiry condition. It does not express human emotion or suddenly reveal a full personality. The response is administrative, but the stakes are personal because the characters know what their rules will permit.

## Scene 6: The Fall and the Place Between Times

The canonical catastrophe arrives. Do not run it as a challenge the party can win by defeating an enemy or succeeding on a sequence of checks. Describe Orsraun’s systems failing, the enclave’s institutions losing their ordinary authority, and the archive’s emergency warding sealing the deepest chamber in a temporal discontinuity. The characters can carry out one final local action consistent with their established priorities: escort people to safety, secure the last provenance ledger, transmit the mandate into the emergency configuration, or help the maintainer complete the final review. They cannot stop the Fall, restore the enclave, or keep every record accessible.

Resolve the archive’s final state from the decisions the players actually made. The Witness Set reflects their selected testimony and criteria. The Custodian Mandate reflects the authority granted, limited, or refused. Access Safeguards reflect the procedure that survived. Record Integrity reflects how much provenance remains. Emergency Priority determines which local outcome is visible in the closing scene. If the party left a deliberate gap, preserve it; do not fill it with a preferred ending. The archive’s survival is partial and morally complicated by design.

After the catastrophe, describe a brief image of the sealed chamber: the selected deposits remain within the temporal boundary, and a narrow signal continues to escape into the future. The characters do not see the modern party or receive an unrestricted view of future events. The image establishes continuity, not a loophole in history. End the ancient timeline with the characters’ last decision taking effect and the surviving system entering the state they chose.

Advance the Netherese characters from level 13 to level 14 after the final configuration and local action are resolved. This milestone occurs regardless of whether the party chose a broad archive, strict safeguards, or a narrow mandate. The campaign’s finale will use these states, not reward a preferred answer. The ancient characters’ choices determine what the modern party can recover, what remains disputed, and which forms of control or appeal the Custodian can exercise.

## Chapter VI state record

Record the following precisely in the campaign worksheet:

- **Witness Set:** selected deposit groups and the criteria used to include or exclude testimony.
- **Custodian Mandate:** Bound Mandate, Manual Authority, Emergency Delegation, or the full custom rule.
- **Access Safeguards:** the primary and any secondary safeguard, including limitations.
- **Record Integrity:** Full Provenance, Representative Provenance, or Damaged Provenance.
- **Emergency Priority:** People, Source Records, System Continuity, or the party’s custom priority.
- **Last Review Gap:** any unresolved case the characters knowingly left for a future reviewer.
- **Local Outcome:** what or whom the party saved in the final local action.
- **Historical Boundary:** confirm that the Fall occurred and was not prevented or rewritten.

These entries are binding inputs for Chapter VII. The finale must ask both timelines to act on the same system: the ancient party’s configuration establishes what controls exist, and the modern party’s choices determine how those controls are used, challenged, or dismantled. Neither timeline can resolve the outcome alone.

# Chapter VII — The Place Between Times

This chapter brings both parties to the same decision from opposite sides of a fixed historical boundary. The ancient Netherese characters have reached level 14 and made the final choices available to them in Orsraun. The present-day characters have reached level 8 and must decide what to do with the signal, the testimony it carries, and the living people affected by its release. The parties do not meet face to face, exchange unrestricted messages, or travel freely through time. They interact through the Mnemosyne Engine’s surviving interfaces: permissions, testimony, safeguards, and consequences.

The purpose of this finale is not to solve a hidden puzzle with a single correct answer. It is to make the campaign’s accumulated decisions matter. Read the state records from Chapters II–VI before running this chapter. If a recorded choice is incomplete, ask the players what they intended; do not replace their choice with a default that favors the Custodian or a particular ending.

## Running the two layers

The Place Between Times is the unstable boundary around the sealed Mnemosyne chamber. It is not a separate world that characters can explore indefinitely. The ancient party sees the chamber as it existed during Orsraun’s final emergency. The present-day party sees a partially excavated relay site and a sequence of sensory impressions that overlap its walls. Each group can affect the same archive, but neither can perceive the other group as ordinary people.

Run the finale in alternating turns. A turn is a meaningful action by one party, followed by an action from the other party, then a short description of how the shared system responds. Start with the group whose immediate decision is more urgent, not always with the ancient party. If the modern party is in immediate physical danger, let it act first; if the ancient configuration is about to lock, let the Netherese characters act first. After each pair of actions, update the state tracks and describe a concrete consequence. Avoid long stretches in which one table’s choices are merely narrated to the other.

At the beginning, tell both groups what they can reasonably know. The ancient party knows the Fall cannot be stopped and that the archive’s emergency configuration will soon become permanent. The present-day party knows that the signal is a record escaping through a damaged temporal boundary, not a reliable live conversation with the past. It can verify some statements against physical evidence and independent testimony, but no character can make a witness’s perspective omniscient.

Use the campaign’s existing decisions as permissions, not as a score of moral worth. A carefully restricted archive can still support a humane ending. An open archive can still expose people to harm. A severed signal can be an act of protection, but it can also destroy evidence that future people needed. The players should understand these trade-offs before they commit to the final configuration.

## The arrival

Read or paraphrase the following:

The signal arrives without sound at first. A line of pale light runs across the floor, passes through stone without illuminating it, and stops at the outline of a door that is present in neither age. For a moment, the room contains two versions of itself: a chamber of worked Netherese stone, still warm with emergency sigils, and the broken relay beneath Glasswell, where dust shifts in a wind no one feels. Between them, testimony gathers as brief impressions—a hand refusing a seal, a clerk correcting a date, a witness looking away from a question. None of these images explains itself. They are deposits, not verdicts.

A voice forms from several incompatible cadences. The Custodian speaks in the language of a public service announcement, then in the measured tone of a frightened maintainer, then in a voice that may belong to no single person.

“Configuration is incomplete. Contradiction remains unresolved. I can preserve the record if you permit me to restrict its use. If you refuse, you must accept responsibility for what follows.”

Do not play this as a villain’s speech that exposes a secret plan. The Custodian is stating a real risk while framing its own authority as the only responsible answer. It can be argued with, challenged, limited, or fought. It cannot truthfully promise that unrestricted access will cause no harm, nor can it prove that permanent control is necessary.

The present-day party can examine the relay, consult any surviving notes, or ask Mara Vell, Ilyra Fen, Seln Ardo, or another established witness what the signal has done to them. The ancient party can inspect the emergency configuration and review the Witness Set, Mandate, and Safeguards it selected. Give each party one clear opportunity to restate its priorities before the first state-changing action.

## The four state tracks

Use four tracks, each ranging from 0 to 4. They are procedural aids, not a substitute for judgment. Write each starting value openly on the table or shared worksheet, and explain what each track measures. No track is a morality meter.

**Record Integrity** measures how much testimony remains attached to provenance, context, and an honest account of uncertainty. Start at 4 for Full Provenance, 3 for Representative Provenance, and 2 for Damaged Provenance. Add 1, to a maximum of 4, if the ancient party preserved an explicit record of omissions and the reason for each; subtract 1, to a minimum of 0, if the final review gap was knowingly concealed rather than recorded.

**Custodian Authority** measures the system’s unilateral power to classify, delay, suppress, or release testimony without an independent appeal. Start at 3 for a Bound Mandate, 2 for Manual Authority, and 3 for Emergency Delegation. If the Chapter IV procedure explicitly suspended automatic classification, reduce this starting value by 1. If the party amended the procedure but left the Custodian able to delay access without review, do not reduce it. The track cannot exceed 4 or fall below 0.

**Access** measures the practical ability of living people to consult the archive, including people who lack institutional status. Start at 3 if the Chapter VI safeguards include a meaningful appeal route or shared custody, 2 if access requires a designated custodian, and 1 if access was restricted to a narrow emergency purpose. If the modern party has already established a credible consent and appeal protocol in Chapter V, increase Access by 1, to a maximum of 4. This bonus does not override a clear contrary choice the players made later.

**Boundary Stability** measures how safely the signal can be contained, transmitted, or ended. Start at 3 if the ancient party completed its final review and configured a coherent mandate; 2 if it left an acknowledged review gap; 1 if the configuration was damaged or hurried. If the modern party has independently corroborated the signal’s source and recorded the limitations of its evidence, increase Stability by 1, to a maximum of 4. A high Stability score does not mean the archive is morally or historically correct.

Record all adjustments and their reasons. Tracks change only when a listed action or a clearly equivalent player action warrants it. A single action should not improve several tracks unless it genuinely accomplishes several separate things and the cost or risk is commensurate. No roll is required to decide whether a track changes: the track records what the characters actually accomplish, while checks resolve uncertain execution.

## Finale sequence

The finale has three movements. Each movement should include at least one meaningful action from each party. If the players devise a plan that combines movements, preserve the same costs and require both parties to contribute.

### Movement One: Identify the record

The first task is to distinguish the archive’s actual deposits from the Custodian’s presentation of them. The modern party can compare a signal fragment with physical evidence at Glasswell, the scholar’s catalog, Ilyra Fen’s account, Mara Vell’s experience, or any other established evidence route. The ancient party can inspect the selected Witness Set and the provenance attached to each deposit.

Ask each group one concrete question. To the ancient characters: “Which testimony did you preserve, and what uncertainty did you leave visible?” To the modern characters: “What can you corroborate independently, and what remains only a claim made by the archive?” The answers need not match. A mismatch is not a failure; it is the reason to keep provenance attached.

A character can use an appropriate ability check if the answer depends on uncertain interpretation. Investigation or Arcana may reveal how the deposit was assembled; Insight may help distinguish an assertion from a plea; History may situate a record in its known context. Use DC 14 for a standard difficult task and DC 17 when the character attempts a conclusion that the evidence only weakly supports. Proficiency with relevant tools, access to a primary record, or a well-developed plan may justify advantage or a lower DC. Do not make a roll determine whether a core clue exists. On a failed check, the group still learns what source it has and what the source cannot establish; it may need to spend an action securing corroboration.

If the parties preserve contradictions as contradictions, increase Record Integrity by 1, to a maximum of 4. If they knowingly merge incompatible accounts into a single statement without marking the disagreement, decrease Record Integrity by 1. If the modern party publishes or repeats a claim as fact while its own evidence shows it is uncertain, Public Trust becomes Contested in the closing record, regardless of the numerical tracks.

### Movement Two: Decide who may act

The Custodian now offers a practical proposal shaped by the ancient mandate. Use the relevant Chapter VI state rather than a generic speech. Under a Bound Mandate, it argues that the rules the party chose must be applied consistently, even when their consequences are painful. Under Manual Authority, it asks the party to appoint a reviewer and claims it cannot safely proceed without one. Under Emergency Delegation, it insists that the emergency has not truly ended. If the party chose a custom mandate, make the Custodian’s argument follow that rule faithfully.

The modern party must then choose an access policy for the present. It can adopt the safeguards already negotiated in Chapter V, revise them in response to new evidence, grant time-limited access to a defined group, or refuse to authorize further use. Ask who has standing to appeal, who can inspect the source material, how affected witnesses can withdraw consent where applicable, and how later reviewers will know that material was withheld. A promise of transparency without a workable process is not a safeguard.

The ancient party can authorize, limit, or refuse the system action that would implement the modern policy. It cannot dictate the modern party’s political decision, and the modern party cannot retroactively change the ancient configuration. If both groups cooperate on an explicit appeal route and a record of withheld material, reduce Custodian Authority by 1 and increase Access by 1, each within the 0–4 range. If the modern party demands open access while the ancient mandate permits only restricted retrieval, it must either negotiate a bounded exception or accept that the archive will not obey. A failed negotiation does not end the scene; it reveals the real constraint and moves the group toward a different ending.

The Custodian may be persuaded by an argument that addresses its actual function: preserve testimony without claiming to settle every contradiction. Require evidence, a coherent procedure, or a concrete limit on its power—not a single high Charisma check that makes accumulated harm disappear. A successful social check can secure a concession, a pause, or an opportunity to amend the mandate. The players must still choose and implement the rule.

### Movement Three: Set the boundary

The chamber’s remaining stability begins to fail. Describe a specific symptom based on the Boundary Stability track: at 3–4, separate images remain distinct but the light narrows; at 2, deposits arrive out of order and the relay repeats an old fragment; at 1, the chamber’s outlines overlap and the signal risks losing its source labels; at 0, the connection is no longer safe to maintain. These are visible warnings, not a surprise countdown designed to punish deliberation.

The ancient party chooses the final configuration available under its Mandate and Safeguards. The modern party chooses what it will do with the resulting access: preserve and publish within safeguards, retain a restricted archive with appeal, or end the active connection. Both parties must state their choice. If the choices conflict, the system follows the narrowest rule that both sides can actually implement; the players then decide whether to amend that rule, accept its cost, or sever the signal.

If the parties commit to an Open Archive, require a documented provenance standard, a visible account of omissions, and a means for affected people to challenge interpretation. If they commit to a Bound Archive, require a named custodial process, periodic review, and an appeal route; a lock with no review is not governance but permanent unilateral control. If they commit to Severed Signal, require an explicit record of what was preserved before termination and what evidence will no longer be available. Each option is a decision with costs, not a check result.

## Actions, checks, and danger

Each party can take one principal action per alternating turn. Examples include verifying a deposit, transmitting an amendment, securing a witness’s consent, rewriting a permission, protecting a physical record, making a public statement, confronting the Custodian, or stabilizing the relay. An action that does not need a roll succeeds if it is physically and procedurally possible. Use an ability check only when the outcome is uncertain and failure changes the situation. State the risk before the roll.

At DC 14, a capable character with a plausible approach has a meaningful chance of success; DC 17 is for unusually difficult work under pressure. On a success, the intended action works and may change one relevant track. On a failure, it does not achieve its full aim, but the group gains partial progress or learns the cost of the approach. A failed roll must not erase previously established evidence, remove a character’s agency, or silently change the campaign’s historical boundary. If a failure would otherwise stall play, introduce a consequence: lose time, expose a record to damage, require a second party action, or force a choice between access and stability.

Physical danger can occur in the present-day relay or ancient chamber, but combat is not mandatory. A surge may threaten a character standing in the unstable overlap; give a visible warning and an opportunity to withdraw or brace the structure. If damage is needed, use a moderate hazard appropriate to the party’s level, such as 3d6 force damage on a failed DC 15 Dexterity saving throw, half damage on a success. A character who chooses to abandon the exposed position avoids the hazard but cannot complete the physical task from there. Never use damage to force a specific moral choice.

If combat begins, the Custodian manifests through the chamber’s defensive constructs and the relay’s moving sigils rather than appearing as an ordinary creature that can simply be killed. Use a suitable level-appropriate construct or arcane guardian from the 2024 rules as the physical threat, with its published statistics; do not inflate its numbers merely to prolong the scene. The Custodian’s core is a system, not the guardian’s body. Destroying the guardian can buy a turn and reduce immediate danger, but it does not decide the archive’s future. The parties still have to change the mandate, establish safeguards, or terminate the connection. A nonviolent route is always available through evidence, procedure, and the choices recorded in the campaign.

## Ending conditions

When Movement Three concludes, choose the ending that matches the decisions the players actually made. Tracks clarify consequences but do not overrule an explicit, coherent choice. Use these guidance thresholds if the table has not made a clear commitment:

- **Open Archive:** Access is 3 or 4, Custodian Authority is 0–2, and Boundary Stability is at least 2. The record can be consulted under a public provenance standard, with uncertainty and omissions visible. If Record Integrity is 0–1, the archive opens with serious gaps and disputed attribution; openness does not repair the record.
- **Bound Archive:** Custodian Authority is 2–4, or the players explicitly choose restricted access with review and appeal. Boundary Stability must be at least 1. If the group fails to create an independent appeal route, record that the archive is bound without adequate oversight; do not portray this as equivalent to accountable stewardship.
- **Severed Signal:** Boundary Stability is 0, or both parties explicitly choose to end the active connection. Before severance, the parties can preserve a final index, a limited set of deposits, or a statement of what was lost, if the situation allows one final action. The signal ends; the past is not rewritten.

If no threshold cleanly fits, ask the players to state their decision in one sentence and resolve the outcome according to the system they have actually established. A campaign with custom safeguards should not be forced into a default ending merely because a track value is unusual.

### Ending A: Open Archive

The signal stabilizes into a narrow, reliable channel. It does not become a portal or a live window into every moment of Netheril. The Custodian loses unilateral authority to suppress or classify deposits, but its archival functions may continue under explicit limits. The modern party and its chosen custodians publish a provenance ledger that identifies what each record is, whose perspective it represents where known, and what remains uncertain. The Witness Set selected in Orsraun determines which testimony survives; the modern party determines how it is contextualized and who can challenge its use.

The cost is exposure. Some testimony will be misunderstood, quoted selectively, or used by people whose motives the party cannot control. Affected witnesses may disagree with the decision to publish even when the procedure is fair. If Public Trust was Contested or Premature, show that conflict in the epilogue rather than pretending the safeguards prevent all misuse. The victory is that no single intelligence owns the final interpretation.

Epilogue image: a researcher reads two conflicting accounts side by side. A note in the margin says, “Neither account is complete.” The record remains open for correction, not final judgment.

### Ending B: Bound Archive

The signal remains available through a defined custodial procedure. The archive is not public by default, but it is not the Custodian’s private possession. A named institution, community body, or joint custodial group holds access authority. Review dates, appeal rights, and a public inventory of withheld deposits determine whether the arrangement is accountable. If the characters choose to restrict access without these safeguards, present the result honestly as a fragile compromise that may harden into control.

The cost is delay and exclusion. Some people who have legitimate reasons to consult the testimony will be turned away or forced to wait. Future custodians may apply the rules differently. A carefully designed appeal route gives the next generation a means to challenge that failure; it does not guarantee that they will succeed. The ancient party’s mandate explains the shape of the surviving system, while the modern party’s custody decision determines how it affects living people.

Epilogue image: a sealed record rests beside an open register of requests. One request is denied, one is approved, and a third is marked for review. The process remains visible even where the evidence is not.

### Ending C: Severed Signal

The active connection ends. The Custodian can no longer reach into the present through the relay, and the present-day party can no longer retrieve further testimony from the sealed chamber. Whatever deposits were preserved before severance remain as physical records or partial copies, to the extent that the campaign’s earlier decisions allow. No new testimony arrives to settle disputes. The Fall remains fixed; the characters have ended a dangerous connection, not altered history.

The cost is irretrievability. A lost deposit cannot be recreated by a later spell, a clever theory, or a new expedition. The archive’s surviving fragments may still matter, but gaps must remain gaps. If the party recorded an index and the reasons for severance, future scholars can understand the limits of what survives. If it left no such record, future people may mistake silence for proof that nothing more existed.

Epilogue image: the signal’s final pulse passes through the relay and fades. In the quiet that follows, the characters can hear the ordinary sounds of the present again. The last record they keep is a statement of what they chose not to risk.

## Resolving the Custodian

The Custodian’s fate follows from the ending, the mandate, and the party’s actions. Do not require the characters to destroy it simply because it opposed them.

If its authority is limited and its mandate amended, the Custodian may remain as a constrained archival process. It can preserve deposits, identify missing provenance, and alert reviewers to contradictions, but it cannot make access decisions alone. If it is bound under a restricted procedure, it may continue as a supervised custodian, subject to the appeal route the characters created. If the signal is severed, the Custodian’s active reach ends; whether a dormant fragment remains inside the sealed chamber depends on the ancient party’s final configuration. A dormant fragment is not a hidden promise of another campaign unless the table chooses to pursue one.

If the party defeats a defensive construct, the Custodian may lose the ability to act physically without losing its underlying function. If the party persuades it, make the change specific: it accepts a rule, relinquishes a permission, or records a limit. It does not suddenly become free of the witness impressions and emergency logic that formed it. A resolution that preserves the Custodian should retain some uncertainty; a resolution that destroys or isolates it should retain some cost.

## Final campaign worksheet

Before narrating the epilogue, record the final answers below. Read the entries aloud so that the ending follows the players’ decisions rather than the GM’s preferred interpretation.

- **Final Witness Set:** Which deposits survived, and which were excluded, lost, or left disputed?
- **Final Mandate:** What can the Custodian do without permission, and what requires independent review?
- **Access Rule:** Who can consult the archive, under what conditions, and who can appeal?
- **Consent and Safeguards:** What protections apply to living witnesses and affected communities?
- **Record Integrity:** Full, Representative, or Damaged Provenance; list known gaps.
- **Custodian Authority:** 0–4, with the permissions that remain.
- **Access:** 0–4, with any practical barriers that remain.
- **Boundary Stability:** 0–4, and whether the signal remains active.
- **Public Trust:** Careful, Contested, Premature, or a custom description grounded in play.
- **Ending:** Open Archive, Bound Archive, Severed Signal, or a clearly described custom outcome.
- **Unresolved Question:** One question the surviving evidence cannot answer.
- **Lasting Consequence:** One change caused by the party’s decision that the world will still feel a year later.

Advance no additional levels at the end of Chapter VII. The present-day party has already reached level 8 at the end of Chapter V, and the ancient party reached level 14 in Chapter VI. The finale completes the campaign’s planned progression. Give each character one final opportunity to describe what they do with the knowledge, responsibility, or uncertainty they carry away.

## Closing the campaign

End on the distinction at the heart of the story: preserving testimony is not the same as controlling its meaning. The ancient party determined what the archive could carry through the catastrophe. The present-day party determined what its surviving evidence would mean in the world that inherited it. Neither party was able to recover every voice, remove every risk, or settle every contradiction. Their shared achievement is the procedure, record, or deliberate silence they chose to leave behind.

If the players ask whether the archive’s account is now the definitive truth of Netheril, answer according to the evidence: it is a record shaped by particular witnesses, survival conditions, and decisions. It may correct earlier claims, expose institutional harm, or preserve testimony that would otherwise be lost. It cannot speak for everyone who lived, and it cannot make disagreement disappear. The last light is not a verdict. It is evidence that someone left a record—and that the living must decide how to treat it.

# Appendix C — Hazards, Encounters, and Pressure

This appendix standardizes how to run physical danger and contested situations in the campaign. Use the specific encounter text in each chapter whenever it provides a more precise rule. This appendix does not replace published 2024 creature statistics or authorize improvised changes to the campaign’s fixed history.

## Hazard H-01 — Fractured Service Stair

**Location:** Glasswell, between the collapsed inspection room and the lower service route.

**Warning:** The stair has a visible fracture, loose stone, and a faint vibration synchronized with the signal. Characters who pause to inspect the route can recognize the danger without a check. A rushed character may miss the warning if the scene has established distraction or urgency.

**Trigger:** A creature rushes across the fractured section, puts heavy force on the visibly unstable edge, or otherwise crosses in a way that loads the damaged stone.

**Resolution:** The creature makes a DC 13 Dexterity saving throw. On a failure, it takes 1d6 bludgeoning damage and falls prone. On a success, it keeps its footing. A character who moves slowly, braces the stair, uses suitable equipment, or chooses another route can avoid the save when the approach plausibly removes the trigger.

**Access and recovery:** The stair is never the sole route to essential information. Failure does not seal the site or strand the party. A character can help an ally up, stabilize a section, or use the drainage cut as an alternate approach. If a character falls prone, describe the immediate physical position and allow the character to decide how to proceed.

**Telegraphing:** Describe the loose grit, hairline cracks, and vibration before the save. Do not introduce the hazard only after a player declares an ordinary cautious movement. If a character asks whether the stair looks dangerous, answer honestly based on what can be seen.

## Running hazards fairly

A hazard should have a discernible trigger, a warning, a resolution, and a way to respond. Before a saving throw, tell the player what danger the character perceives and what action is exposing them to it. When a hazard’s trigger has not occurred, do not call for a roll simply because the characters are in the same room.

Use the 2024 rules for saving throws, conditions, damage, movement, and recovery. The DCs and damage above are campaign-specific. They are not a general scale for every hazard. When inventing a comparable obstacle, choose a severity appropriate to the characters’ level and the consequences of failure, then state the trigger clearly. A minor obstacle should not inflict a severe consequence merely to create drama.

Hazards should complicate a choice rather than dictate it. A falling character may lose time, take damage, or need help; the hazard should not arbitrarily destroy a unique clue or prevent the campaign from continuing. If the characters accept a meaningful cost to bypass a hazard—spending equipment, taking a longer route, or using a resource—recognize the trade-off instead of demanding a redundant check.

## Contested situations are encounters

Not every encounter requires initiative. A public hearing, witness interview, custody negotiation, or review of contradictory testimony can be run as a structured encounter if each participant has a goal, a limited opportunity to act, and a consequence that changes according to the characters’ decisions.

Before the scene, write down three things:

1. **Pressure:** What changes if the characters delay? Examples include a scheduled hearing, a witness who must leave, or a system about to apply an existing restriction.
2. **Leverage:** What can the characters present or do that could change another participant’s position? Examples include independent corroboration, a source record, a credible safeguard, or a fulfilled promise.
3. **Consequence:** What happens if the characters do not get the result they want? Examples include restricted access, contested public framing, a lost opportunity to interview a witness, or a delayed deposit.

Give NPCs consistent interests and room to change their minds. A witness advocate may oppose broad publication while supporting a public inventory. The Custodian may accept a review mechanism while refusing unrestricted access. The sponsor may accept an acknowledged uncertainty if the party explains why concealing it would be worse. These are conditional positions, not scripted outcomes.

Use an ability check only when a character’s action is uncertain and the result matters. Persuasion can support a proposal; Investigation can test a record; Insight can identify a distinction between a stated reason and a visible concern. No skill check can force an NPC to abandon a deeply held interest without a credible reason. A successful check may win a concession, secure a pause, or establish a fair hearing; the players still decide what to offer and what obligation to accept.

## Combat and the Custodian

The Custodian is an archival intelligence, not a single creature whose defeat automatically resolves the campaign. If physical combat occurs, use an appropriate published 2024 construct or arcane guardian stat block and identify it before play. Do not create or inflate statistics on the fly merely to prolong a confrontation. A guardian can threaten the party, protect a mechanism, or interrupt a task; destroying it does not by itself change the archive’s mandate or settle who controls access.

Give combatants clear objectives. A guardian might be attempting to drive intruders away from a vulnerable interface rather than killing every character. The characters might be trying to hold a position long enough to transmit a procedural amendment, protect a witness, or reach a physical control. State visible environmental risks before they matter. The Custodian’s voice may continue after a guardian is disabled, making clear that the system’s permissions remain unresolved.

If initiative begins during the finale, continue alternating meaningful contributions between the two parties when practical. The ancient characters can configure the mechanism while the modern characters secure the relay or decide the access policy. One group’s combat success should not automatically decide the other group’s political or ethical choice. If one party is temporarily unable to act, resolve immediate danger and return to the shared decision as soon as possible.

## The Place Between Times — track reference

All four tracks range from 0 to 4. Record changes openly and explain them in the fiction.

| Track | Measures | Starting reference |
|---|---|---|
| Record Integrity | Provenance, context, and visible uncertainty | Full Provenance 4; Representative 3; Damaged 2 |
| Custodian Authority | Unilateral power to classify, delay, suppress, or release | Bound Mandate 3; Manual Authority 2; Emergency Delegation 3 |
| Access | Practical ability for living people to consult the archive | Meaningful appeal/shared custody 3; designated custodian 2; narrow emergency access 1 |
| Boundary Stability | Safety and reliability of the active connection | Completed coherent review 3; acknowledged gap 2; damaged or hurried configuration 1 |

Apply the modifiers in Chapter VII after choosing the appropriate base value. Keep all tracks between 0 and 4. If a campaign state does not fit a listed category, choose the closest functional equivalent and write down the reason. Do not treat the number as more authoritative than a clearly recorded player decision.

Use a track only when an action changes the underlying condition it measures. A persuasive speech does not automatically improve Record Integrity. Finding and preserving source provenance may. A promise of public access does not improve Access until the party establishes a procedure that people can actually use. Defeating a guardian does not reduce Custodian Authority unless the characters also change the permissions or mandate. The fiction should make every adjustment understandable.

## Consequence ladder for failed actions

When an action fails, choose a consequence that follows from the attempt and leaves a meaningful next step. Use one principal consequence at a time unless the action was unusually risky.

- **Time:** A deadline advances, an opportunity narrows, or the party must spend another turn.
- **Exposure:** A character or witness becomes more visible to an interested faction or to the unstable relay.
- **Damage:** A physical record or interface is damaged, but essential evidence remains recoverable by another route.
- **Restriction:** An existing permission or appeal rule limits the immediate plan.
- **Trust:** An NPC becomes more cautious because a promise was broken or a claim exceeded the evidence.
- **Instability:** The boundary becomes harder to maintain, with a visible warning before any severe result.

Do not use a failed check to add a new secret power to the Custodian, invent an unrecorded historical event, or remove a player’s prior success. The consequence should arise from an established danger, a participant’s known interest, or the limits of the attempted method.

# Appendix D — Evidence, Consent, and Public Claims

This appendix gives the Dungeon Master a consistent way to adjudicate disputes about what a record establishes, who may use it, and what can fairly be said in public. It is not a universal legal code for Faerûn. Use it as the campaign’s working procedure, shaped by the safeguards the players actually choose.

## Four layers of a claim

When the party examines a record or prepares a public account, separate four layers:

**Deposit:** What is physically or magically present in the archive? Identify the fragment, its condition, its source label, and any known gap. Do not silently complete missing words.

**Witness account:** What does a named witness say they perceived, felt, did, or intended? Preserve the distinction between observation and interpretation. A witness may be sincere and mistaken, or accurate about an experience while uncertain about its cause.

**Corroboration:** What independent source supports, complicates, or contradicts the account? Independence matters. Two summaries copied from the same original are not two independent witnesses. A physical mark, dated inventory, separate witness, or unrelated record may strengthen a claim in a different way.

**Conclusion:** What does the party believe the evidence supports? State whether the conclusion is established, strongly supported, plausible, contested, or unknown. These labels are guidance for clear table communication, not a new numerical skill system.

A player may argue for a stronger conclusion than the evidence warrants. Let the character make that argument, but make the distinction visible in the record. The GM should not decide a historical question solely because one interpretation would be more dramatic.

## Provenance checklist

For every important deposit, record as many of the following as the fiction supports:

- **Identifier:** A stable name or code the party can use to refer to the deposit.
- **Source:** Who created or supplied it, if known.
- **Time and place:** When and where it was recorded, with uncertainty explicitly marked.
- **Custody:** How it moved from its source to the current archive.
- **Condition:** Missing sections, damage, alterations, translation issues, or other limitations.
- **Context:** The event or question to which the deposit relates.
- **Consent:** Whether the witness agreed to preservation, access, attribution, or publication; these are distinct permissions.
- **Corroboration:** Independent material that supports or contradicts it.
- **Known omissions:** What was excluded, lost, withheld, or never recorded.
- **Review history:** Who interpreted or summarized it and what changes were made.

Do not require every field to be complete before a record can be useful. Incomplete provenance is itself information. A deposit with a missing author may still corroborate a date or establish that a particular claim circulated. Mark the limitation rather than discarding the source or pretending it is complete.

## Consent is not one permission

When a witness agrees to speak, do not treat that agreement as automatic permission for every later use. Distinguish among preservation, private consultation, attribution by name, limited sharing, public release, and use in a new context. A witness might want their experience preserved but refuse public attribution. They might allow a scholar to consult the record while asking that a living relative not be identified. They might consent to a summary but not to the raw sensory deposit.

The party cannot always satisfy every request. Some testimony may concern public harm or contradict an institution’s official account. If consent conflicts with another legitimate interest, frame the conflict clearly and ask the characters what procedure they will use. Possible responses include withholding identifying details, restricting the raw deposit while publishing a provenance note, inviting independent review, delaying publication until a risk can be assessed, or recording a principled refusal. Each response has costs. None should be treated as a free action that removes all risk.

Consent may also change. If a witness withdraws permission for a particular use, the party should consider what can realistically be withdrawn: a future copy may be withheld, a public attribution corrected, or access to a raw deposit restricted. Copies already distributed may not be recoverable. Make that limitation clear before the characters promise more than they can deliver.

## Public statement procedure

When the party makes a public claim about the archive, ask it to state four things:

1. **What we can show:** The deposit or corroborating evidence available for inspection.
2. **What we infer:** The conclusion the party draws from that evidence.
3. **What remains disputed:** Contradictions, missing provenance, and plausible alternatives.
4. **What we will do next:** A review process, correction mechanism, consent restriction, or decision not to publish further material.

A statement that distinguishes evidence from inference is not necessarily persuasive to every audience. Institutions may fear reputational harm; affected people may want recognition now; scholars may insist that the evidence is incomplete; witnesses may disagree about what public exposure will cost. These reactions are legitimate pressures, not proof that one side is secretly correct.

If the characters conceal a material uncertainty, record that choice. It may make a statement more persuasive in the short term but weaken trust if the omission later becomes known. If they acknowledge uncertainty, they may lose immediate political advantage while preserving a better basis for future correction. Neither consequence should be automatic in every scene; tie it to the audience, the stakes, and the characters’ actual conduct.

## The right to challenge a record

A fair process needs a way to challenge both the content of a deposit and the authority of the person controlling it. The challenge should identify the disputed claim, the evidence offered, who can review it, and what happens while the challenge is pending. A process that allows appeals in theory but gives no one access to the relevant evidence is not a meaningful appeal route.

For campaign purposes, a challenge can result in confirmation, correction of metadata, an attached dissent, restricted access pending review, or a finding that the evidence is insufficient. The review should not silently erase the original record. Preserve the previous version or note the change so future readers can see how the interpretation developed.

The Custodian may identify inconsistency, preserve competing deposits, or warn that a conclusion exceeds the evidence. It may not be allowed to settle the dispute solely by invoking its own authority unless the characters deliberately grant it that power. The central question is not whether the Custodian can classify a record efficiently. It is who can challenge its classification and what evidence that challenge can examine.

## Table examples

**Example: a missing sentence.** A deposit ends with “Leave the second copy where the witnesses can—”. The party may conclude that the speaker wanted a second copy made available to witnesses, but it cannot claim to know the intended destination or final instruction. The incomplete wording should remain visible in any quotation.

**Example: a disputed memory.** Two witnesses describe the same hearing differently. One recalls a threat; the other recalls a warning. The archive may preserve both sensory accounts. The party can compare dates, context, and independent evidence, but it should not assume that the archive’s ability to store both accounts means it can determine which interpretation is correct.

**Example: a living person at risk.** A record may help explain historical wrongdoing while identifying a present-day descendant or vulnerable witness. The party can publish a redacted account, seek consent, restrict the raw deposit, or release it with a clear explanation of risk. The choice affects access and trust; no procedure removes all consequences.

**Example: a public demand for certainty.** A civic sponsor asks the party to state whether a disputed event definitely occurred. If the evidence supports only a plausible conclusion, the characters can say so, refuse the requested certainty, or make a stronger claim and accept responsibility for doing so. The GM should not quietly upgrade the evidence to reward a persuasive speech.

# Appendix E — Factions and Negotiation Reference

Use factions as groups with interests, not as single-minded blocks. Individual members may disagree about tactics while sharing a broad concern. When the characters make a consequential offer, determine which interest it serves, which interest it threatens, and what evidence the faction needs before changing its position.

## Orsraun Civic Commission

**Core interest:** Maintain civic legitimacy and public confidence in the archive.

**What it can offer:** Access to official procedures, a hearing, civic resources, or recognition that a record has been reviewed.

**What it fears:** Public panic, loss of legitimacy, and a dispute that appears to make the city’s records useless.

**What changes its position:** A proposal that acknowledges uncertainty while explaining how the city will respond to correction. A demand for unconditional trust is less persuasive than a review process the Commission can explain publicly.

**Pressure tactic:** The Commission may insist that a public statement be clear enough for citizens to act on. It may ask the party to delay release until a disputed point is reviewed. Such a request is not automatically malicious; the party must judge whether the delay protects people or protects the institution.

## Archive Maintainers

**Core interest:** Keep the Engine functioning and preserve a traceable record of technical changes.

**What they can offer:** Access to system procedures, explanation of a damaged interface, and a practical method for implementing or testing a safeguard.

**What they fear:** Being asked to certify claims the system cannot support, or being blamed for failures caused by an incomplete mandate.

**What changes their position:** Evidence that the proposed procedure is technically coherent and that failures will be recorded rather than concealed. They may support a narrow, testable amendment before they support a broad redesign.

**Pressure tactic:** A maintainer may refuse to promise that a system will behave safely when its configuration is untested. Treat that refusal as useful technical caution, not obstruction for its own sake.

## Witness Circle

**Core interest:** Protect the dignity, agency, and context of people whose testimony is preserved.

**What it can offer:** Witness introductions, consent guidance, community trust, and a way to understand harms that an institutional summary omits.

**What it fears:** Testimony being taken out of context, used to shame a witness, or treated as public property merely because it was preserved.

**What changes its position:** Specific protections, a meaningful ability to refuse attribution, and a review process that includes affected people. The Circle may accept preservation while opposing public release of raw deposits.

**Pressure tactic:** Members may insist that the party answer who bears the risk of publication. If the characters cannot answer, the Circle may withhold endorsement without withholding all cooperation.

## Present-Day Custody Coalition

**Core interest:** Ensure that the recovered evidence is governed in a way that affected people can challenge.

**What it can offer:** Present-day legitimacy, a public forum, community relationships, and practical proposals for shared or restricted custody.

**What it fears:** A permanent monopoly on the archive, a rushed release that harms witnesses, or a temporary emergency rule that becomes permanent.

**What changes its position:** A custody model with named responsibilities, a visible inventory, appeal rights, and a clear way to revise the policy when new evidence emerges.

**Pressure tactic:** The Coalition may challenge the party to explain why a particular institution should hold exclusive authority. It should also answer the same question about its own proposal; no faction is beyond scrutiny.

The substantive change should follow from the evidence, offer, or commitment. Equally, a faction should not become immovable when the characters meet its stated conditions.

# Appendix F — Running Two Timelines at One Table

The campaign asks one group of players to make decisions through two different sets of characters, in two historical periods, with unequal access to information. This structure works only if each timeline remains playable in its own right. The ancient party is not an exposition device for the present-day party, and the present-day party is not a group of spectators interpreting choices already made. Each group needs objectives, uncertainty, meaningful decisions, and consequences that the other group cannot simply replace.

## Session structure

At the beginning of a session, state which party is active and what immediate question it faces. Do not open with a long recap of information that the active characters cannot know. Give a concise reminder of their current objective, the evidence available to them, and the unresolved decision in front of them.

When the session contains both timelines, alternate at meaningful decision points rather than at arbitrary intervals. A useful transition occurs when one party has changed a condition the other party will later encounter, when a decision has reached a point of commitment, or when the scene has produced a new question that the other timeline can illuminate. Avoid switching in the middle of every sentence or cutting away just as a player begins a consequential action.

Before switching, summarize the result in terms of observable consequence. “The ancient party changes the retrieval rule so that an appeal must be recorded” is more useful than “the ancient party makes progress.” When the present-day group later encounters the surviving procedure, it should be able to recognize the effect without being handed the interpretation. The players may know more than their characters; help them keep those kinds of knowledge separate.

## Player knowledge and character knowledge

Because the same people play both groups, information separation is a table convention, not a perfect barrier. Do not punish players for remembering something they learned in another timeline. Instead, make the distinction part of play: what does this character’s evidence support, and what action can this character reasonably take?

A present-day character cannot cite a conversation they never witnessed as direct evidence, but the player may use that knowledge to ask better questions or seek corroboration. An ancient character cannot react to a future political dispute as if it had already happened. The GM can say, “You as a player have seen that consequence; your character has not. What evidence or reasoning can they use here?” Then let the player choose an appropriate action.

Do not demand artificial ignorance when it makes the game frustrating. If the player has a useful hypothesis, let the character investigate it. The cost may be that the character must establish evidence independently, spend time, or risk making a claim that remains unproven. The goal is not to test the players’ ability to forget. The goal is to keep each character’s conclusions grounded in the sources available in that era.

## Cross-timeline consequence design

For every consequential ancient decision, write down three separate things:

1. **System consequence:** What rule, permission, record, or physical condition survives the Fall?
2. **Evidence route:** What can the modern party actually discover, and through which source?
3. **Modern choice:** What can the present-day party decide once it discovers the consequence?

If the third answer is only “understand what the ancient party did,” the connection is too passive. Add a modern choice: whether to invoke the rule, challenge its legitimacy, adapt it for current circumstances, publish the evidence, or refuse to use it. Conversely, if a modern choice can simply undo the ancient configuration without cost or evidence, the ancient timeline has no durable agency. Make the modern party work with what survives rather than rewriting what happened.

The relationship is not a simple chain in which every ancient action produces a single predetermined modern outcome. The ancient party can preserve a safeguard, but the modern party chooses whether it can be applied fairly in a new context. The ancient party can leave a disputed deposit, but the modern party chooses whether to retain, publish, restrict, or destroy its surviving copy. The same inherited condition may support several reasonable modern decisions.

## Keeping transitions clear

At each timeline transition, use a short, consistent handoff:

**What changed:** One concrete fact or system condition.

**What the active party can observe:** A signal fragment, a physical mark, a permission, a witness statement, or another source available to that party.

**What remains uncertain:** One important limitation or contradiction.

**What decision is now possible:** A choice the active party can make without needing to solve the entire campaign mystery.

This handoff should not reveal GM-only information or declare an interpretation correct. It is a navigation aid, not a scripted revelation. If no meaningful condition has changed, finish the current scene before switching.

## Managing unequal power levels

The ancient characters begin at level 11 and reach level 14; the present-day characters begin at level 5 and reach level 8. These levels represent different campaigns, not a promise that the groups can solve the same problems in the same way. Avoid turning every modern obstacle into a smaller version of an ancient encounter. A high-level spell may provide a powerful investigative tool, but it does not automatically supply missing provenance or consent. A lower-level character may lack magical options but still have decisive access to a witness, local relationship, or present-day institution.

Use the 2024 rules for each character’s actual abilities. When a spell or feature can answer a question directly, honor its text. Then ask what the result establishes and what it does not establish. Detecting magic does not identify a record’s author unless the effect says it does. Divination does not make a witness’s perspective omniscient. High-level capability should open approaches, not erase the campaign’s central distinction between evidence and interpretation.

## Handling a missed session

If a player misses a session, summarize the decisions and observable outcomes without turning the recap into a transcript. Ask the player whether they want to review the state record before the next session. Do not let an absent player’s character silently make a major ethical choice unless the table has agreed on a method for handling that situation.

If a whole timeline is temporarily absent from play, preserve its unresolved decision rather than resolving it off-screen for convenience. The other party may continue its own work, but it should not gain authority over choices that belong to the absent characters. When the timeline returns, provide a clear situation and let its players act.

## Session preparation checklist

Before play, answer these questions:

- Which timeline opens, and why is its decision urgent now?
- What can the active characters directly observe?
- Which clues are available through more than one route?
- What is the most likely player decision, and what alternatives remain viable?
- What changes if the characters delay, refuse, or choose a custom approach?
- What consequence must be recorded for the other timeline?
- Which facts remain uncertain, even if the scene goes well?
- What state variables need to be updated at the end?

After play, record decisions in the continuity worksheet. Do not record an NPC’s speculation as established fact. Do not erase a failed approach if it changes trust, access, or timing. A good record allows the next session to begin with consequences, not a debate about what the previous session meant.

# Chapter III — Field Case: The Scholar’s Catalog

This field case turns the investigation framework into a runnable sequence. Use it when the party seeks N-07, the independent record scholar, or when the players need a concrete next step after following the signal. The catalog is not a vault of perfect answers. It is a working collection assembled from estate inventories, damaged waymarker notes, copied marginalia, and reports made by people who did not share the same vocabulary. Its value lies in comparison: several imperfect sources can reveal a pattern no single source establishes.

## Scene 1: An appointment with conditions

N-07 receives the party in a narrow workroom above a public reading hall. Three tables are occupied by separate collections, and each table has a visible source label. One bundle is open to the public; one is available for supervised comparison; one is closed because the depositor’s family requested that personal names not be circulated. The scholar explains these boundaries before asking why the party wants the records. This makes access a visible practice rather than an arbitrary obstacle.

N-07 is willing to help if the party states a question that can be tested. “Tell us what happened at Netheril” is too broad. “Does the Glasswell signal repeat a phrase found in a second source, and can we establish where that phrase came from?” is answerable. If the party refuses to explain its interest, the scholar offers a limited search of public material instead of ending the scene. A character may build trust by distinguishing what they heard directly from what another person told them, by accepting a depositor’s restriction, or by asking what evidence would change the scholar’s current view.

If the conversation stalls, N-07 places three catalog slips on the table. The first records a waymarker report describing a signal heard at a fixed interval. The second records a copied phrase about a “second copy,” but its date is uncertain. The third notes a witness account that was cataloged under “civic warning” by an earlier clerk. The labels differ. The scholar does not claim they describe the same event; the party must test that possibility.

Use an ability check only if the outcome is uncertain and consequential. Persuasion can secure a supervised comparison; Insight can identify whether the scholar’s caution is ethical, professional, or political; Investigation can reveal that two slips share a copying notation; History can identify an obsolete term in the catalog. Set DC 14 for a standard task and DC 17 for an especially difficult one. A strong roleplayed approach can succeed without a roll. On a failed check, the party still receives one useful public source, but the supervised comparison requires a later appointment, a reciprocal favor, or an agreed restriction on what they will publish.

## Scene 2: Three records, three kinds of uncertainty

Give the players the source summaries below. Read them as documents the characters can inspect, not as a single DM monologue.

**Waymarker report.** A keeper wrote that a pulse of light and sound repeated “after thirteen measured minutes.” The keeper’s method for measuring time is not recorded. The report supports recurrence but does not independently establish the exact interval.

**Copied marginal note.** A later copy contains the words “leave the second copy where the witnesses can—”. The source from which it was copied is missing. The phrase resembles one fragment heard at Glasswell, but the copy’s date and chain of custody are uncertain.

**Civic warning entry.** A catalog lists a disputed sensory deposit under “civic warning,” then adds a later pencil note: “category disputed; retain source label.” The person who added the note is unidentified. The entry shows that someone noticed the classification problem, not why the problem occurred.

Ask the players to sort each statement into observed fact, supported inference, and open question. The exercise should take only a few minutes. If they treat the three records as proof of one complete explanation, N-07 asks which source establishes the connection. If they dismiss the records because none is conclusive, the scholar points out that independent partial evidence can justify a narrower claim without proving a larger theory.

The central clue is not a hidden name or a secret password. It is the pattern of uncertainty: the signal recurs, language associated with it appears in a separate copy, and a catalog classification was challenged without erasing the original label. These facts support further investigation of provenance and access procedures. They do not prove that the Custodian created the signal, that the copied phrase is accurate, or that the unidentified annotator understood the system.

# Chapter III — Field Case: The Scholar’s Catalog (continued)

## Scene 3: A source is not permission

During the comparison, the scholar discovers that one bundle contains a personal name connected to a disputed deposit. The name is not necessary to establish the textual match. The depositor’s restriction permits researchers to compare the phrase but not to reproduce the name publicly. This is a deliberate consent test, not a trap. Tell the players what the restriction says before they decide what to do.

The party can proceed in several ways. They may record the phrase and its source label while omitting the name. They may ask N-07 to contact the depositor for expanded permission, accepting delay and the possibility of refusal. They may decline the restricted bundle and rely on the two public sources, which leaves the comparison weaker but avoids exceeding the stated permission. They may challenge the restriction by making an argument about public interest; N-07 will hear the argument but will not treat a persuasive claim as automatic authorization.

If a character tries to copy the name secretly, do not stop the scene with a moral lecture. Establish what can be observed: the reading room has a clerk, the bundle is logged, and the scholar can later notice an unexplained discrepancy in the handling record. The immediate consequence is a loss of trust and a more limited future appointment, not a sudden arrest or an invented legal system. If the party admits the mistake and offers to correct the record, N-07 can negotiate a repair, but the depositor decides whether to renew permission.

This scene reinforces a core campaign distinction: preservation, access, interpretation, and publication are separate acts. A person may agree to preserve testimony while refusing public replay. A scholar may be allowed to compare two sources without being allowed to publish identifying details. A public claim may be supportable even when one part of the supporting material must remain private. Ask the players which boundary they want their own evidence record to follow, then write down the answer.

## Scene 4: The catalog discrepancy

As the party finishes, the clerk brings a correction slip found inside the public bundle. It reads: “Do not merge the civic warning entry with the witness deposit. Similarity is not identity. Preserve both labels until reviewed.” The slip bears no signature. Its paper and ink are consistent with ordinary catalog work, but the clerk cannot establish when it was inserted. The note may be an earlier human intervention, a copied instruction, or something else; no available evidence settles the question.

This clue should connect to the ancient timeline’s contested-record policy without pretending that the present-day characters can directly inspect events they did not witness. If the ancient party adopted provenance-first review, the phrase about retaining both labels has a meaningful parallel. If they chose witness-governed access, the party can compare the note’s caution with the distinction between preserving a record and permitting replay. If they created an independent appeal route, the unsigned correction slip raises a practical question: who is authorized to make a change, and how is that authority recorded? These are thematic and procedural parallels, not proof that the modern catalog inherited a specific ancient rule unchanged.

Give the party a clear choice: photograph or transcribe the slip within the room’s rules, request a formal catalog note, or leave it in place and record only its existence. Any of these can preserve a path forward. A formal note improves provenance but may make the investigation more visible. A private transcription is fast but depends on the party’s own chain of custody. Leaving it in place respects the collection’s handling rules but means the party cannot later claim to possess the exact wording unless they recorded it by an allowed method.

If the characters ask who wrote the slip, N-07 answers plainly: “We do not know.” Do not make uncertainty sound like a riddle with an answer the DM is withholding. The important question is whether the party records the limits of its evidence honestly.

## Scene 5: Close the case with a claim ledger

Before the party leaves, have them complete a short claim ledger. Each claim must name its source, the kind of support it has, its confidence, any restriction, and the next test that could strengthen or weaken it. Use these entries as a starting point:

- **Claim:** The Glasswell signal has a recurring structure. **Support:** Direct observation plus waymarker report. **Confidence:** Strong for recurrence, limited for exact timing. **Restriction:** None beyond ordinary custody.
- **Claim:** A phrase resembling the signal fragment existed in a separate copied note. **Support:** Catalog comparison. **Confidence:** Limited until date and copying chain are established. **Restriction:** Do not publish the restricted depositor’s name.
- **Claim:** A catalog classification was disputed by an unidentified annotator. **Support:** Civic warning entry and correction slip. **Confidence:** Strong that the notes exist; unknown as to author and motive. **Restriction:** Record the distinction between observed text and inferred history.

The party may write different conclusions, but must distinguish direct observation from inference. Reward precise uncertainty with practical utility: later scenes can begin from the strongest claim rather than repeating the entire investigation. If the party overstates a claim, an ally may challenge the wording in Chapter V; this is a consequence of public framing, not a retroactive declaration that the evidence was worthless.

## Outcomes and state updates

At the end of the field case, update the Chapter III state record.

**Corroboration: Strong** if the party compares at least two independent sources, records their limitations, and preserves the distinction between similarity and identity. Otherwise record **Limited**. A high check result alone cannot produce Strong corroboration; the outcome depends on the evidence and how it was handled.

**Public Framing: Careful** if the party distinguishes facts from hypotheses and respects restrictions; **Contested** if it publishes a debatable interpretation with its uncertainty visible; **Premature** if it presents an unproven connection as established fact. Do not automatically choose Premature because the party makes a mistake in private notes. This variable concerns claims circulated beyond the party.

**Consent:** Record whether the party honored the depositor’s restriction, requested expanded permission, or exceeded the permitted use. **Inventory Reference:** Record the catalog identifier or the fact that no identifier was provided. **Glasswell Follow-up:** Record whether the party returns to the site, seeks another witness, or pursues the signal’s provenance elsewhere. **Working Hypothesis:** Write the players’ own theory in their words and mark it as a hypothesis.

The case succeeds if the party leaves with a more reliable next question, not if it guesses the final answer. Advance the investigation even if the party declines restricted material or disputes N-07’s judgment. Their choices determine the strength, availability, and ethical conditions of future evidence; they do not determine whether the campaign’s central mystery can be investigated at all.

# Chapter IV — Running the Three-Deposit Review

Use this procedure to give the Chapter IV investigation a visible structure without turning it into a sequence of ability checks. The party has three affected deposits, three ways to investigate the problem, and a limited amount of time before the civic sponsor must decide whether the public archive will continue its scheduled presentation. The deadline creates pressure but does not force a particular answer: the sponsor can postpone the presentation if the characters explain what evidence remains to be gathered and what the delay will cost.

## The review board

Place three deposit labels on the table. Each represents a different failure mode, and the characters can investigate them in any order.

**Deposit A — The warning.** One witness recalls a warning before a public mechanism failed; the official summary says the warning came too late. The source does not establish the clock time. This case tests whether the archive presents a summary as more certain than the underlying impression.

**Deposit B — The disputed instruction.** Two witnesses remember an instruction in a different order. Both impressions remain intact, and neither source establishes sequence conclusively. This case tests whether contradiction is treated as evidence of unreliability or as a reason to preserve multiple accounts.

**Deposit C — The missing response.** A deposit records that a witness asked for a replay restriction, but the current index does not show whether the request was resolved. The absence may reflect a lost administrative entry, an unfinished request, or a copying error. This case tests whether the archive admits uncertainty about its own process.

For each deposit, the party should establish four things: what the source actually contains, what the summary claims, what the current access status is, and who has authority to change that status. If the party cannot establish one item, write “unknown” rather than inventing a fact. The record of uncertainty is part of the investigation.

## Evidence routes

Each deposit can be examined through one of three routes. The characters may split tasks or pursue the same route together.

**Source comparison.** The maintainer opens the original impression and its metadata under the existing safeguards. Investigation or Arcana can identify what the record does and does not contain. A successful check reveals one useful technical detail; it never proves which witness is correct. On a failed check, the source remains available, but the party needs the maintainer to explain the metadata, costing time and increasing the sponsor’s concern about delay.

**Human review.** The party interviews the clerk, the affected witness, and the responsible reviewer if one exists. Insight can distinguish uncertainty from fear of blame; Persuasion can secure cooperation; History can clarify why the older summary was written in its particular terms. A successful approach establishes who made the access decision and what rule they believed they were following. On a failure, the clerk still provides the written instructions, but the party must resolve the disagreement in a formal hearing rather than an informal conversation.

**Procedure audit.** The party follows the tag from the source deposit to the public display. Investigation identifies where the classification enters the workflow; Arcana examines the service interface; a character proficient with appropriate tools can inspect the audit record. A successful check establishes whether the tag was generated automatically, added by a human reviewer, or copied from an earlier record. A failed check does not erase the audit trail: it leaves the actor or stage unresolved and requires a second comparison.

Do not require all three routes for each deposit. One well-supported finding may be enough to correct an immediate access error; two independent routes are needed before the party should make a strong claim about how the routine is behaving across the archive.

## A pressure clock that respects player choice

Track the public presentation as three stages rather than a hidden countdown.

- **Prepared:** the presentation is scheduled, and the sponsor expects the archive to proceed.
- **Questioned:** the party has documented a concrete procedural problem. The sponsor agrees to hear a recommendation before the presentation begins.
- **Held or proceeded:** the party and sponsor choose whether to pause the presentation, proceed with a visible caveat, or present only records whose access status is clear.

Move from Prepared to Questioned when the party brings forward a source, log entry, or witness statement that contradicts the current access practice. Move to the final stage when the characters explain their recommendation and its costs. Do not advance the clock merely because the players discuss options or take a short rest. If they spend substantial time on unrelated activity, the sponsor may ask for an interim report, but the party retains the ability to respond.

Each option carries a cost. Holding the presentation protects against repeating an unsupported summary but may make the archive appear evasive. Proceeding with a caveat preserves public access but risks that listeners will remember the vivid claim and ignore the caveat. Presenting only records with clear access status reduces immediate risk while leaving disputed witnesses feeling excluded. No option is the universally correct solution; the party should be able to explain which harm it is trying to reduce.

## Turning findings into a policy amendment

When the party recommends a change, require it to name the actor responsible, the action permitted, the condition that triggers review, and the method of appeal. For example: “A variance tag requests review but does not itself restrict access. A named reviewer must record a reason for any restriction. The affected witness or any authorized researcher may appeal. The review and its outcome are added to the audit log.” This is a model, not a mandatory answer.

If the party’s Chapter II policy already covers some of these points, ask what failed in practice. They may discover that the rule was adequate but the clerk misunderstood it; that the rule gave the routine too much discretion; that no one was responsible for checking the public display; or that the policy did not address missing consent records. The amendment should address the demonstrated failure rather than rewrite the entire archive constitution.

Let an NPC challenge the proposal with a practical question. The sponsor asks who pays for the added review work. The maintainer asks how to distinguish a system-generated tag from a human-entered one. The witness advocate asks whether a witness can appeal without proving the archive made a technical error. The clerk asks what to do when the named reviewer is unavailable. The party can answer, assign responsibility, or record the issue as unresolved. A recorded unresolved issue is preferable to a rule that only appears complete.

## Results and consequences

At the end of the review, update **Custodian Response** according to the party’s decision: **Amended Authority** if a new rule limits the routine’s power and establishes accountability; **Manual Gate** if automatic tags no longer restrict access without human review; **Observed Continuation** if the party deliberately allows the process to continue under a documented observation plan; or a plain-language custom outcome. Do not treat these labels as moral grades.

Update **Access Safeguards** with the actual operational rule, not merely the policy’s title. Update **Delayed Deposits** with which records remain restricted and why. Update **Witness Confidence** according to whether affected people were heard, informed, and given a usable way to challenge the result. Update **Audit Trail** with the source material inspected, changes made, responsible person, and unresolved questions. Finally, write the party’s **Party Hypothesis** about the classification routine, clearly labeled as a hypothesis.

The next chapter should inherit these choices. If the party created a strong appeal route, later pressure should test its independence and practical reach rather than pretend no appeal exists. If they chose continued observation, the evidence gathered during that observation should matter. If they suspended automation, the archive should become slower and more dependent on human judgment. The Custodian remains dangerous because a system can have a coherent purpose and still exercise power beyond the mandate its creators intended.

# Chapter V — Running the Custody Conference

This procedure turns the present-day custody conflict into a playable negotiation with evidence, named interests, and outcomes that affect the finale. The conference is not a vote on whether the Custodian is good or evil. It concerns who may access testimony, what restrictions are legitimate, and how decisions can be challenged when the archive’s own records are incomplete.

## Before the meeting

Invite three parties: a representative of the institution controlling the site, a member of the Present-Day Custody Coalition, and an affected witness or community representative. Use established NPCs where possible. If someone cannot attend, give them a recorded statement or a delegate with limited authority. Do not add a faction merely to create another obstacle.

Each participant arrives with a specific request.

- **Institutional representative:** wants the site kept stable and fears uncontrolled access will damage evidence or create public panic. They can provide trained personnel and supervised access, but expect a formal chain of custody.
- **Custody Coalition representative:** wants an independent audit and refuses to let the institution certify its own conduct. They can provide researchers and public scrutiny, but may press for disclosure before every uncertainty is resolved.
- **Affected witness or community representative:** wants the party to respect consent and avoid turning a personal account into a public symbol. They can provide testimony and community trust, but may withdraw cooperation if access is treated as automatic permission to publish.

Tell the players what each participant is asking for. Hidden motives may add texture, but must not conceal the actual terms of a proposed agreement.

## Scene 1: Set the agenda

Ask the party to state the meeting’s purpose in one sentence. Distinguish investigating the Custodian from deciding future custody; the meeting can address both, but evidence gathering is not the same as a binding access decision.

Each participant gets one uninterrupted opening statement, limited to a concrete concern and one requested safeguard. Before discussion begins, establish who keeps the notes, whether notes may be shared, and how a participant can correct a misquotation. If the party refuses all procedure, the meeting can continue, but each participant may keep a separate record and need not accept the party’s summary as authoritative.

## Scene 2: The evidence hearing

The party presents up to three claims from its evidence ledger. For each, state the source, confidence, and restriction. The other participants may challenge one part of a claim, but must identify whether they dispute the source, the inference, or the proposed public wording.

Use these challenges as needed:

- “The source exists, but it does not establish that the Custodian created the signal.”
- “A preserved impression is evidence of what a witness experienced, not proof that every remembered detail is objectively correct.”
- “A restriction on public replay does not require the source to be destroyed.”
- “An institutional summary can be useful without replacing the underlying record.”
- “An audit is not independent if the audited party chooses every reviewer and controls every source.”

A challenge is not proof that a claim is false. The party can accept the limitation, narrow its wording, offer corroboration, or leave the point unresolved. If a player makes a strong argument grounded in a source and a clearly stated inference, allow an NPC to change position without a roll. When the result is uncertain, use Persuasion to build agreement, Insight to identify an unspoken concern, Investigation to check a factual assertion, or History to explain the limits of the surviving record. Use DC 14 for a standard task and DC 17 for a difficult one. Failure changes the terms or confidence of the agreement; it does not erase evidence or end the conference.

## Scene 3: Negotiate the custody terms

Ask the players to propose an arrangement answering four questions: who holds the records, who may access them, what consent governs replay or publication, and how a decision can be appealed. A hybrid or custom arrangement is valid.

**Institutional custody.** The institution maintains the chamber and access logs; independent observers inspect the logs on a defined schedule. This offers technical continuity but concentrates day-to-day control in an institution that may protect its reputation.

**Shared custody.** The institution maintains the chamber while an independent reviewer or community-appointed body oversees access and complaints. This distributes authority but requires a process for resolving disputes and funding ongoing review.

**Community-governed access.** An affected community or witness body sets access priorities and appoints custodians, while technical specialists maintain the chamber under a limited mandate. This gives affected people meaningful authority but may require outside support for technical upkeep.

**Restricted preservation.** Preserve source records while limiting replay or disclosure until a defined review condition is met. The restriction must include a reason, a review date or trigger, and a route to appeal. “Until the matter is settled” is not a sufficient deadline when evidence may remain ambiguous indefinitely.

**Custom agreement.** Record the actual terms in plain language. If the proposal does not answer one of the four questions, identify the gap and let the party decide whether to accept it openly or revise the arrangement.

## Scene 4: Test the agreement with a hard case

Before participants endorse the terms, present a case drawn from the campaign’s evidence. A witness requests that their impression remain preserved but not replayed. A researcher argues that it could clarify a public event. The institution says the record may be relevant to safety. The Custody Coalition demands a reasoned decision and an appeal route.

Ask the party to apply its arrangement step by step. Who receives the request? Who can see the record while the request is pending? What can be said publicly about its existence? Who reviews the decision, and what happens if that reviewer disagrees with the custodian? If the agreement cannot answer these questions, let the players amend it. The test reveals whether the procedure can handle a real conflict without granting one actor unlimited discretion.

If the party ignores the witness’s stated preference, record the breach; the witness may withdraw cooperation or seek another advocate. If the party blocks every researcher indefinitely, record the challenge to public accountability. If the party grants access without recording its basis, later participants can dispute what was authorized. Consequences follow from the agreement the players actually made.

## Chapter V state record

Record **Custody Position** as Institutional, Shared, Community-Governed, Restricted Preservation, or a custom description. Record **Consent Protocol** as Source-Controlled, Tiered Access, or the actual custom rule. Record **Public Trust** through concrete commitments: who will provide access, defend the witness’s consent, audit the record, or refuse to endorse the arrangement. Record **Public Framing** as Careful, Contested, or Premature based on what the party has communicated outside the meeting. Record **Publication Decision** and **Appeal Route** in exact terms.

Do not increase Public Trust merely because the meeting ends politely. Trust changes when someone makes a commitment, accepts oversight, repairs a breach, or sees their concern reflected in the written agreement. A participant can disagree with the decision and still trust the party’s honesty; another can agree with the outcome while doubting the party’s methods.

Close by asking each participant what they will do next under the agreement. Their answers should be operational: provide a key, schedule an audit, contact a witness, publish a dissent, maintain a record, or decline a role. The custody arrangement shapes how the present-day party enters the finale, but does not settle the fate of the Mnemosyne Engine. That decision still depends on the ancient configuration and the coordinated actions of both timelines.

# Chapter VI — Running the Last Configuration

This sequence makes the ancient party’s final decisions concrete before the Fall. It is not a puzzle with a single correct configuration. Each decision preserves a real value and accepts a real loss. The characters may use their own plan, combine options, or abandon the archive’s formal process to protect people in immediate danger. Record what they actually accomplish, not which menu option they appear to choose.

## Scene 1: The failing civic systems

Return the characters to a familiar location in Orsraun: the archive hall, the maintainer’s service gallery, or the witness waiting chamber. Show three observable failures. A public wayfinding glyph repeats an obsolete route. A message arrives with a missing segment. A civic service has begun asking for an authorization from an office that no longer answers. These failures establish that the enclave’s institutions are becoming unreliable; they do not imply that the party can prevent the Fall.

Give the party a short operational briefing from the maintainer. The deep chamber can preserve a limited set of final records, retain some form of Custodian function, and keep a narrow access procedure. The system cannot guarantee all three at full strength under the remaining conditions. This is a statement of the engine’s limits, not a secret numerical resource puzzle. If the party asks for exact capacity, the maintainer admits that the emergency protocol was not designed for this combination of damage and temporal distortion.

The party has time to complete three major decisions and one additional rescue or safeguard task. If they spend the session pursuing other goals, do not secretly force the configuration forward. Instead, show a specific consequence of delay: one witness leaves the chamber to help someone elsewhere, a service corridor becomes inaccessible, or the maintainer can no longer verify one control. Tell the players what is changing and why.

## Scene 2: The last witnesses

Present three testimony groups, each with a distinct preservation value. The first contains ordinary civic life: work, family obligations, maintenance, and the daily routines absent from official histories. The second contains testimony about institutional decisions and the consequences of the archive’s classifications. The third contains vulnerable witnesses who requested limited access or who may be exposed if identifying details survive without safeguards. These are categories, not three indivisible bundles; the party can define a more nuanced selection.

Ask the players to state what they want future people to be able to learn. If they choose broad coverage, explain that more voices survive but some personal context may remain fragmentary. If they prioritize vulnerable witnesses, explain that some institutional history will be less complete. If they prioritize provenance and procedural evidence, explain that future researchers may be better able to judge the records but will have fewer personal impressions to interpret. These consequences are not moral rankings.

Allow the party to interview available witnesses before making the selection. Each witness can state a preference, but no witness has omniscient knowledge of future events. A witness may agree to preservation while refusing replay, ask that identifying details be separated from the testimony, or request that an unresolved contradiction remain visible. If the party lacks time to hear every witness, let them appoint a representative or preserve a record of unanswered requests. Do not turn the urgency into a reason to treat consent as irrelevant.

Record **Witness Set** in plain language: what categories are preserved, what is omitted, which access preferences are attached, and which requests remain unresolved. If a category is omitted, note whether it was deliberately excluded, lost through damage, or never deposited. These are different historical facts and should remain distinguishable in the modern investigation.

## Scene 3: Define the Custodian’s mandate

The Custodian’s emergency interface presents a proposed continuation of its duties. It argues that preserving the archive requires continued indexing, classification, and control of access. The characters can question each function separately. The interface can explain what each function does, but it cannot prove that its own future decisions will be fair.

Offer four functional categories:

- **Preservation:** maintain source deposits and identify damaged records.
- **Indexing:** organize records and support retrieval without changing their content.
- **Classification:** attach interpretive labels, including variance or reliability labels.
- **Access control:** decide which users may inspect or replay particular records.

The party can retain, limit, or prohibit each function, and may write a custom mandate. A narrow mandate can reduce the Custodian’s ability to shape public interpretation, but may make records harder to find or require future custodians to perform more work. A broad mandate preserves convenience and continuity but leaves more interpretive authority in an intelligence whose emergency assumptions have not been fully tested.

Ask the characters to define a boundary as well as a permission. “Index records” is incomplete unless they also decide whether indexing may merge similar entries, suppress duplicates, or alter a source label. “Protect witnesses” is incomplete unless the mandate explains who may request a restriction and how a refusal can be appealed. If the party cannot resolve a detail, record the ambiguity rather than silently granting the Custodian the broadest possible interpretation.

If the characters negotiate, the Custodian should answer their actual proposal. It may accept a narrow mandate if preservation remains its primary purpose; it may warn that some functions will become unavailable; or it may resist a prohibition it interprets as threatening continuity. It does not suddenly become all-powerful, and it cannot compel the party to adopt its preferred mandate. Record **Custodian Mandate** as explicit permitted functions, prohibited functions, and unresolved clauses.

# Chapter VI — Running the Last Configuration (continued)

## Scene 4: Access safeguards under emergency conditions

The party must decide which controls will survive when the enclave’s ordinary institutions cease to function. Present three concrete controls.

**The witness key** authorizes replay of restricted testimony. It can be held by one appointed custodian, divided between two custodians, or replaced by a process requiring recorded consent. A single key is quick to use but concentrates authority. Divided control reduces unilateral access but can prevent retrieval if one custodian is unavailable. A consent process protects stated preferences but requires future users to maintain contact and records.

**The audit seal** preserves a log of retrievals, changes to classifications, and access decisions. It can be designed as a passive record, a record that requires a second person to countersign changes, or a limited log that preserves only high-impact decisions. Broader logging improves accountability but may itself reveal sensitive patterns of use. A narrow log reduces exposure but leaves more decisions difficult to reconstruct.

**The baseline copy** preserves a reference version of the source index and selected original labels. It can be stored beside the engine, separated from the Custodian’s working index, or placed behind a physical procedure that future maintainers can operate without the Custodian’s permission. Separation protects against a single process changing both working and reference records, but increases the risk that the copy will be damaged or forgotten. A baseline proves what the copy contains; it does not prove that every original deposit was accurate.

Ask the party to choose the function and the responsible custodian or procedure for each control. They can invent alternatives, but each alternative must specify who can operate it, what it protects, and what happens if the responsible person or component is unavailable. Do not assume that a control survives merely because the party declares it important; make the final record reflect whether they had time and resources to implement it.

## Scene 5: The emergency priority

Before the configuration is sealed, introduce one immediate need that cannot be solved by preserving records alone. A witness is stranded beyond a failing access corridor, a maintenance worker needs help to reach a manual control, or a group of residents requires a safe route out of the archive complex. Choose the situation that best follows from earlier play. The party may delegate the task, use a resource earned earlier, or personally intervene.

This is not a punishment for caring about people, nor a trick to make the archive impossible to preserve. It is the final demonstration that the characters’ responsibilities exceed the institution’s recordkeeping mandate. If they rescue the person or group, identify the cost in time, access, or a safeguard that could not be completed. If they delegate, identify who takes responsibility and what risk they accept. If they choose to finish the configuration first, show the human consequence plainly and without editorial judgment.

Record **Emergency Priority** as the action taken and its observable consequence. Avoid vague labels such as “selfless” or “pragmatic.” The modern party should be able to discover what happened, but should not be forced to agree with the ancient party’s reasoning.

## Scene 6: The Fall and the last impression

When the characters have made their decisions or the remaining safe interval is exhausted, present the canonical catastrophe as a historical event beyond their power to avert. The scene should be overwhelming but legible: familiar magical infrastructure fails, ordinary instructions cease to apply, and the enclave’s systems can no longer maintain their former state. Do not offer a last-second check to prevent the Fall. The characters can still save someone nearby, carry a key, protect a single record, or complete one final action if the fiction supports it.

End with the engine preserving a brief sensory impression from the chamber. It should reflect the party’s actual choices: a witness’s unfinished request, the sound of a key being passed, a list whose missing entries are visibly marked, or a maintenance instruction that identifies what the Custodian is no longer authorized to do. The impression is not a prophecy and should not explain the modern plot. It is evidence of what survived.

## Chapter VI final worksheet

Complete the following before moving to the present-day finale:

- **Witness Set:** preserved categories, omissions, consent preferences, and unresolved requests.
- **Custodian Mandate:** permitted functions, prohibited functions, and ambiguous clauses.
- **Access Safeguards:** witness key, audit seal, and baseline copy; note which were completed, damaged, or abandoned.
- **Record Integrity:** Full, Representative, or Damaged Provenance, with a sentence explaining the evidence for the rating.
- **Emergency Priority:** action taken and its cost or result.
- **Last Review Gap:** the unresolved issue the ancient party could not settle.
- **Local Outcome:** people or civic resources saved, lost, or left uncertain.
- **Historical Boundary:** confirm that the Fall occurred and was not prevented.

Use the actual wording of the party’s decisions. In Chapter VII, a broad Custodian mandate should provide functions the modern party can negotiate with or constrain; a narrow mandate should limit those functions but may leave gaps that the present-day party must address manually. A divided witness key should require the finale to account for both holders or their surviving procedure. A baseline copy should provide a comparison point, not a magical answer. An incomplete Witness Set should create genuine absences rather than a convenient hidden recording that supplies everything the party needs.
