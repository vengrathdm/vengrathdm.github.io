A Second Moon Over the Eastern Quarter?

Photographers disagree about the light above the rooftops. The observatory has refused to publish its latest measurements.

By Dr. C. VeyFrom our own correspondentsHeartwell edition

Seventeen minutes of pale light

Reports of a second moon began arriving at the newsroom shortly after the first frost. At first, the accounts were dismissed as reflections from the gasworks. The witnesses, however, describe a disc that remained visible when the lamps were extinguished and the shutters closed.

Three photographs have been submitted for examination. In each, the disc appears above the same group of chimneys. The plates were made with different cameras and at different times. None shows the object when viewed from beyond the eastern wall.

The Royal Observatory has declined to comment. A clerk, speaking privately, says that the instruments registered a shadow crossing the city from below rather than above.

The instruments recorded an eclipse. The sky above the city remained clear.

The notice in red ink

The phenomenon has produced a lively market in protective lenses, silvered mirrors and bottled night air. Several vendors have been fined for selling counterfeit charms. The Society for Practical Astronomy has advised the public not to look directly at the disc, though it has not explained what harm such an act might cause.

A note delivered to this office bears the observatory seal and a warning in red ink: THE SKY IS NOT THE SOURCE. The handwriting has not been identified.

This edition will be distributed only in the eastern districts. Readers elsewhere may request a copy through the usual booksellers, provided they are prepared to wait.

From the editor's desk

The Observatory's public ledgers are closed until further notice. The last entry available for inspection is dated three nights before the first sighting.

Notice to readers
